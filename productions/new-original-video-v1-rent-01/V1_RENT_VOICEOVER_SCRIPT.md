In the year to September, the official US rent index rose 2.8 percent.
Zillow's own measure of typical market rent rose 2.7 percent over the same twelve months.
Two different measures from two different sources, almost the same answer. So if both say about three percent, why does rent feel like a bigger number than that? Because they are answering a different question from the one you are asking. This video is about what a rent number actually measures, and what it leaves out.

Every month the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index. Rent is one piece of it.
Look at the weights. In the August 2026 weights, the item called rent of primary residence is 7.7 percent of the whole index. Owners' equivalent rent, the rent a homeowner would pay for their own home, is another 25.9 percent. Together, with a few smaller items, the BLS group called shelter is 35.3 percent of the entire CPI.
So the rent number is not a side detail. About a third of the inflation figure that reaches the news is housing. When the rent measure moves slowly, headline inflation moves slowly with it.

Here is the method, from the BLS own explanation of how it measures rent.
The BLS does not look at every apartment. It takes a sample of rental units. Each sampled unit is priced every six months, because a lease fixes the rent for a term. The sample is split into six groups, called panels. One panel is priced in January and again in July, the next in February and August, and so on. So every month, one sixth of the sample gets a new price.
Then comes the arithmetic. The monthly change is the sixth root of this ratio: today's average rent in the panel divided by the average rent for the same units six months earlier. A sixth root turns a six month change into a one month change. Those monthly changes are chained together to build the index.
Two more details. If a unit is vacant when the BLS checks it, the BLS estimates its change using units that were newly rented. And each new price carries an adjustment for the age of the unit.
Notice what this means. The BLS number is a measure of price change for a fixed set of homes, observed at six month steps. It is not the average price of a home on the market this week.

Now take Zillow's index. In October 2026 Zillow's smoothed US rent index stood at $1,913. A year earlier it was $1,859. That is a rise of 2.9 percent. In September it was 2.7 percent.
Here is the thing to take away. Zillow's figure is a level, a dollar amount, and a change in that level. The BLS figure is a change only. The CPI does not say what the typical rent is. It says how fast prices moved for a group of homes.
That is why "rent is high" and "rent is rising fast" are two different claims. A dollar amount can be uncomfortable even when its yearly change is 2.8 percent. A 2.8 percent rise on a rent that is already stretched is still a bigger monthly bill.

We went looking for a clean story, the one that says the official number is stuck behind the market. We did not find it.
First, the BLS explanation tells you the collection cycle, six months. We looked for a BLS sentence that says the CPI rent trails market rents. We did not find one.
Second, on the numbers we could read, the two measures sit close together: 2.8 percent for BLS in September, 2.7 percent for Zillow. In October the BLS rent index rose 0.2 percent for the month; Zillow's index was almost flat that month.
Third, the BLS did not print a twelve month rent change in its October release, so there is no October to October comparison from the BLS to put next to Zillow's 2.9 percent.
So the honest answer is narrower than a headline. The official rent number moves in six month steps, it matches the market measure today within a fraction of a point, and it cannot tell you the dollar price of renting.

If you read a rent headline, ask three questions. Is it a level or a change? Is it measured on the same homes or on new listings? How often is each home priced?
If you are comparing your own rent with any of these numbers, a change in an average does not say what you will be asked to pay. It describes the market, not your lease.
This is not financial advice. It describes how two published measures are built. Sources are in the description, and every number on screen carries its source tag.
