## Voiceover

The Moon vanishes. No explosion, no falling debris, just an impossible switch that removes the Moon and its gravitational influence from our ocean experiment. You're standing on a beach as the tide comes in. Does the water suddenly stop?

No. Water already moving has momentum. Removing a force that helps drive the tide doesn't instantly erase the motion the ocean already has. Waves and currents would continue to move and interact with the shape of the coast. Working out exactly what happens next would require an ocean model; it won't be the same at every beach.

First, what was the Moon doing to the water? Earth holds the ocean close with its own gravity. The Moon doesn't lift the whole ocean off the planet. Instead, its gravity pulls slightly differently on different parts of Earth. Those differences help drive long waves through the ocean.

A simple picture shows two broad bulges, one on each side of an ocean-covered Earth. That's useful for understanding the direction of the tide-generating effect, but our real planet has continents, shallow seas, deep basins and a rotating surface. Water can't move through a continent just because a diagram has a bulge there.

Instead, tidal waves travel through ocean basins and meet coastlines. They can be delayed, redirected and amplified in some places. A local high tide is the result of that whole moving system, not simply the Moon passing overhead.

Now remove the lunar forcing. The existing ocean motion does not vanish at the same instant. It would evolve, interact with shores and lose energy through friction and other processes. Without a simulation, we shouldn't assign a universal height or a precise countdown to that adjustment.

But the Moon was not the only celestial body pulling unevenly on Earth. The Sun also produces a tide-generating effect. So after our impossible switch, a solar-driven tide remains. The timing and size at a particular coast still depend on the ocean's shape and movement; they cannot be read directly from a comparison of solar and lunar forces.

There would also be a change in the pattern we know today. When lunar and solar effects line up, they can reinforce each other. At other alignments they partly oppose each other. Remove the Moon and that familiar spring-neap relationship disappears, even though the Sun continues to drive water.

And tides aren't the only reason the sea moves. Wind makes waves and currents. Storms can raise water along a coast. The Moonless ocean would not become a perfectly level mirror waiting for a replacement moon.

What about the long-term climate, Earth's tilt, or the impossible physics of removing a celestial body? Those are different investigations. Here we're asking what happens to the ocean when lunar tidal forcing is removed, while treating the rest of the setup as fixed.

The answer is neither an instant still sea nor one guaranteed worldwide flood. A major driver disappears, water already in motion keeps moving, and the Sun remains. The coast's next tide would be the outcome of an ocean adjusting, not a switch flipping from 'tides on' to 'tides off.'
