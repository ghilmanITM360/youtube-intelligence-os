## Voiceover

You jump sideways on a tiny world. The ground curves away beneath your feet. You keep falling toward it, but the ground keeps turning out of your way. Could you circle the whole world without ever landing?

Let's build a world where we can actually follow the question. It's a perfectly smooth ball of rock, three kilometres from its centre to its surface, with a uniform density of two thousand kilograms per cubic metre. It doesn't spin. There's no atmosphere, no nearby planet and nothing else pulling on you. Real asteroids are messier; this one exists to make the physics visible.

On Earth, a jump carries you forward for a moment before gravity brings you back down. To stay in orbit, you need enough sideways motion that, as gravity bends your path downward, the ground curves away at the same rate.

That doesn't mean you've escaped gravity. Gravity is what bends the path. Without it, you would travel away in a straight line. With too little sideways speed, the ground catches you. With the right speed and enough clearance, you keep falling around the world.

For our rock, a circular path just at the surface would need about two point two metres per second sideways. That's roughly eight kilometres an hour, around a jogging pace. One lap would take about two hours and twenty minutes.

That sounds promising. But look closely at the drawing: the circle touches the ground everywhere. It's a mathematical boundary, not a comfortable flight path. Your feet, a pebble, or a small error could make contact. A perfectly smooth world is already doing a lot of work for us.

There's another catch. The number is a required velocity, not proof that you can produce it. Gravity here is incredibly weak, so your shoes press against the ground with very little force. That makes pushing sideways a separate problem. Our calculation simply grants you the launch.

And a jump usually sends you upward as well. Suppose you leave the surface with some upward motion and not enough energy to escape. Gravity bends your trajectory back. On this ideal world, the path meets the surface again. The rest of the mathematical ellipse would pass through solid rock, which is an awkward place to continue a flight.

Could we fix that? Start a little above the surface and give yourself just the right sideways speed there. Now a circular path can clear the rock all the way around. But we've supplied the height and the carefully chosen velocity; an ordinary jump hasn't automatically provided either.

Go faster and the path changes again. At a high enough speed, you can leave our isolated world's gravity behind instead of looping around it. For this model, that escape threshold at the surface is about three point two metres per second. A real small moon or asteroid could behave differently because of its shape, spin and neighbours.

So could one sideways jump put you in orbit? Our tiny world needs a speed a human might recognise, but speed alone is not the whole answer. From the surface, the perfectly circular path has no room to spare. Give the traveller a little altitude and the right motion, and the fall can go all the way around. The trick is keeping the world just out of reach.
