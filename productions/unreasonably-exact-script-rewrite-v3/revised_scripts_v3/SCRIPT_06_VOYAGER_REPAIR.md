## Voiceover

Your spacecraft is sending messages from beyond the planets. The radio signal is there, but the information inside has become unreadable. You can't go there, you can't swap a part, and when you send a question you may wait almost two days for an answer. How do you begin a repair?

In late 2023, this was the problem facing the Voyager 1 team. The spacecraft could still receive commands, but the stream coming back no longer contained usable engineering and science data. For the people responsible for a spacecraft launched in 1977, that made even a basic question difficult: what condition is it in?

Voyager has several computers with different jobs. One of them, the flight data subsystem, collects and packages information before it goes to the radio system. If the packaging goes wrong, a working transmitter can faithfully send a message that makes no sense.

The team had to investigate remotely. In March 2024, engineers sent a command intended to prompt a different response from that computer. The response included a readout of its memory. Reading it was like getting a glimpse inside a machine that could no longer explain itself clearly.

The readout pointed to damaged memory. NASA said roughly three percent of the flight data subsystem's memory had been corrupted, and engineers suspected a single chip was no longer working properly. They couldn't establish exactly why. A particle strike and the wear of decades in space were possibilities, not a confirmed diagnosis.

They couldn't replace the chip. So they looked for a way around it.

Part of the computer's software was stored in the affected area. The team found that the code would not fit as one piece in any single available location. Instead, they split it into sections and placed them in different parts of the remaining memory. Other parts of the program had to be adjusted so the pieces could still find and use one another.

This is delicate work on any computer. On Voyager, the distance changes the rhythm of every experiment. In 2024, a signal took about twenty-two and a half hours to travel each way. Even if the spacecraft responded immediately, the signal journey alone meant roughly forty-five hours between a command leaving Earth and the answer arriving. Preparing, scheduling and analysing the response could add more.

In April, the team sent the first part of the fix. When the answer came back, Voyager was again sending readable engineering data. That mattered because it gave the team a way to assess the spacecraft's health while continuing the recovery. Science data came back in stages later; the first response did not mean every part of the job was finished.

Since then Voyager has travelled farther. In November 2026, NASA confirmed it had reached one light-day from Earth. A simple command and immediate reply around that distance take about two days in travel time alone. NASA also reported a later data issue; we shouldn't assume it had the same cause as the 2024 fault.

The repair wasn't a mechanic reaching a broken machine. It was a team learning to communicate around a damaged piece of memory, one carefully prepared exchange at a time. At this distance, patience isn't a personality trait. It's part of the engineering.
