## Voiceover

Point a powerful laser at a reflector on the Moon and send a pulse. How long before its light comes home?

About two and a half seconds for the round trip. Not each way: the pulse takes a little over a second to get there, and a little over a second to return. The exact time changes as the Moon's distance changes.

Apollo astronauts left special reflectors on the lunar surface. Their corners redirect a small fraction of the incoming light back toward its source. A telescope on Earth sends pulses and looks for the incredibly faint return.

Most of the light doesn't make it back to the detector. Scientists collect the few returning photons from many pulses and account for effects such as Earth's atmosphere. That lets them measure the distance with remarkable precision.

The basic idea is a cosmic stopwatch: multiply the round-trip time by the speed of light, then divide by two. The Moon isn't just hanging in the sky. With each faint return, we can measure where it is.
