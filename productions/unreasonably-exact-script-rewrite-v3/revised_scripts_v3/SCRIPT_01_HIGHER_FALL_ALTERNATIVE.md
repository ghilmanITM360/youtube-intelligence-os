## Voiceover

Two identical objects fall toward the same patch of ground. One starts much higher than the other. Which one hits faster?

The higher one seems like the obvious answer. It has farther to fall, so gravity has more time to speed it up. And in one very important version of this experiment, that's exactly right.

Remove the air. Drop both objects from rest. With nothing pushing back, the object released higher reaches the ground faster. The extra distance gives gravity more opportunity to increase its speed.

Now put the air back, and watch what happens to the speedometer.

At first, the falling object speeds up. But it also has to push air out of the way. The faster it moves through that air, the greater the drag opposing its motion. Eventually, if conditions remain steady, drag can balance the object's weight. The object is still falling, but its speed stops increasing. That's terminal speed.

Now raise the starting point. Once an object has had enough distance to get close to that speed, adding more height in the same steady air gives it more time falling, but doesn't add much speed at the bottom. The distance keeps changing. The final speed can approach a limit.

That is already a different answer from the airless experiment. But our atmosphere doesn't have the same density all the way up. High above the ground, the air is thinner. It offers less resistance at a given speed.

Let's use a deliberately simple model: the same object, the same fixed orientation, no wind, and an atmosphere that gets denser closer to the ground. Release it high enough and it can accelerate through thin air to a speed greater than its eventual speed near the ground.

Then it enters denser air. The drag grows, and for part of the fall it can become stronger than the object's weight. The object is still moving downward, but its downward speed decreases. Its peak speed and its speed near the ground are two different entries on the scoreboard.

So a higher starting point can mean a higher peak, a longer fall, and still a similar speed near the bottom in this particular model. The object's earlier journey matters, but the air it passes through later can change its speed again.

There are limits to this story. Real bodies rotate and change shape. Weather varies. At very high speeds, a simple fixed-drag calculation becomes less reliable, and heating and other effects matter. An object may not remain intact, and a model of impact speed cannot tell us whether anyone would survive.

Even the phrase 'hit harder' needs care. Impact speed is only one part of an impact. Mass, the surface, and how quickly the object stops all matter too.

So does a higher fall always hit harder? No. In a vacuum, under our simple assumptions, more height gives more impact speed. In an atmosphere, drag can make the relationship very different. A longer fall can reach a greater speed in the sky without arriving at the ground faster. The last part of the journey has a vote too.
