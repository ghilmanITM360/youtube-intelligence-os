## 0:00 — Cold open `[SIMULATION]`
*Tracker: ALTITUDE 400 km · SPEED 0. A suited figure hangs above a thin blue limb. Two empty lines wait on the HUD: PEAK and LANDING.*

You step off into space.

Four hundred kilometres up. Nothing under your feet.

For four minutes, nothing happens. Then you hit the air at more than eight thousand kilometres an hour.

*Cut to black.*

Everyone knows the rule: fall from higher, and you hit the ground harder.

So here's the test. One metre, a hundred metres, all the way back up to here. Every time, we write down the speed you land at. Somewhere on this ladder, the rule breaks.

*Title card.*

Does a higher fall always hit harder?


## 0:31 — Heights 1 and 2: one metre, a hundred metres `[THE MATH]`
*The ruler draws itself, 1 m at the bottom, 400 km at the top. LANDING line climbs: 16 → 138. A ghost bar beside 100 m: "NO AIR 159".*

One metre: you land at about sixteen kilometres an hour. Not enough time to finish saying "whoa".

A hundred metres: a hundred and thirty-eight. With no air it would be a hundred and fifty-nine, so the air has started pushing back. It's the only safety equipment you have, and you didn't even have to pack it.

So far, the rule holds. Enjoy it. It's about to stop.


## 0:56 — Height 3: one kilometre, where the rule breaks `[THE MATH]`
*The LANDING line hits ~204 and freezes. Two arrows, WEIGHT down and AIR up, grow until they match. Beside the ruler: "300 m → 187 · 1 km → 204".* `[EXCERPT → Day 2 Short starts]`

From three hundred metres you land at a hundred and eighty-seven. From one kilometre: two hundred and four.

The faster you fall, the harder the air pushes back. At about two hundred kilometres an hour it pushes exactly as hard as gravity pulls, so you stop speeding up. Skydivers call that terminal velocity, which is a worse name than it deserves.

So drop from three kilometres, or five, and you land at about the same speed. Higher doesn't hit harder. It just takes longer.

*Three small cards, original illustrations, no footage. Label: REAL DATA.*

That's how some people have survived huge falls. In 1943, American gunner Alan Magee fell about twenty-two thousand feet without a parachute, through the glass roof of a railway station, and lived. The air had capped his speed. The floor did the rest.

That's luck, not a plan, and nothing here is safety advice.


## 1:45 — Height 4: ten kilometres `[THE MATH]`
*Tracker: the PEAK line starts to rise while LANDING stays flat. SPEED readout: 320 → 265 → 204. A thermometer chip reads −50 °C.*

At ten kilometres the air is thin and about minus fifty. Thin air pushes back less, so you peak higher: about three hundred and twenty kilometres an hour, twenty seconds in.

*The SPEED readout peaks, then falls while the figure keeps dropping.*

Then thicker air brakes you, and near the ground you're back to about two hundred.

Peak speed climbs with height. Landing speed doesn't. `[EXCERPT ends]`


## 2:06 — Height 5: thirty-nine kilometres, the real record `[REAL DATA]`
*Tracker: 39 km. The label switches to REAL DATA. A date chip: 14 OCT 2012. Original illustration of a suited figure on a small platform.*

Thirty-nine kilometres. This one isn't a simulation.

On the fourteenth of October 2012, Felix Baumgartner stepped off a capsule at almost thirty-nine kilometres, where the air is about two hundred and sixty times thinner than at the ground.

About fifty seconds later, near twenty-eight kilometres up, he reached one thousand, three hundred and fifty-eight kilometres an hour. Faster than sound.

*Split panel. REAL: 27.8 km, 50 s. MODEL: 27.5 km, 51 s. Label: THE MATH.*

This is our test. We tuned one number in the model: his top speed. His peak height and timing came out of the physics, within about three hundred metres and a second. That's a consistency check, not proof.

And the model has a flaw. It keeps him faster than sound for thirty-six seconds. In reality it was thirty. So trust everything above this height to about plus or minus fifteen percent, and less the higher we go.


## 2:55 — Height 6: one hundred kilometres `[SIMULATION]`
*Tracker: 100 km. The label switches to SIMULATION and stays there. A dashed line labelled "100 km: a convention". PEAK keeps rising; LANDING stays flat at 204.*

Above here, nobody has jumped. It's all model.

A hundred kilometres is the line the international body that certifies these records uses for the edge of space. Space has a border the way a map does: handy on paper, invisible when you're there.

Drop from it and you'd peak near three thousand six hundred kilometres an hour, with the air braking you at about five g. That's five times your own weight, pressing on you.

Five and a half minutes later, you land at about two hundred. The same as from one kilometre. Still flat.


## 3:29 — Height 7: four hundred kilometres `[SIMULATION]` `ESTIMATE`
*Tracker: back to the cold-open frame, now with numbers. G readout rises to 20, with a bar "> 10 g for ~11 s".*

Back to the top. Four hundred kilometres. There's almost nothing to fall through, so you pass a hundred kilometres at about eight thousand four hundred an hour.

Then the air hits. The braking peaks near twenty g and stays above ten for about eleven seconds, while the air ahead of you is heated to thousands of degrees. Those are estimates, and shaky ones: up here the air is so thin that our simple drag model is at its least trustworthy. Think of them as educated guesses in a lab coat.

Rocket-sled riders have taken forty g and more, but only for a second or less. Twenty g for seconds, with that heat, is outside any test we know of. We can't tell you how it ends.

If you got through, the model says you'd land at about two hundred, like everything else on this ladder. The word "if" is doing a lot of work there.


## 4:24 — The answer `[THE MATH]`
*The full ruler with every height lit. The LANDING line is flat from 1 km up to 400 km; the PEAK line climbs.*

So, does a higher fall always hit harder?

Up to a few hundred metres, yes. From about a kilometre, no: the air caps your landing at around two hundred kilometres an hour, whether you start at three kilometres or a hundred.

And from about thirty-five kilometres up, the danger moves into the sky itself: faster than sound, then crushing g, then heat.

Higher doesn't mean a harder landing. It means a longer, stranger fall, with more time to reconsider.


## 4:53 — One more thing: orbit `[REAL DATA]`
*The cold-open frame again, 400 km. A space-station silhouette (generic, original drawing) crosses it sideways, right past the falling figure.*

One more thing. The International Space Station flies at about this height. It isn't up there because it's high. Things at four hundred kilometres fall.

It stays up because it moves sideways at about eight kilometres every second, falling the whole time while the ground curves away.

Same height. Same gravity. Only one of you is going sideways.
