# V2 Airfares: draft script (DRAFT 1, 2026-11-18, not approved for production)

News-window video: publish only within about a week of a fresh primary release (next BLS CPI: 2026-12-10). Re-read every number at publish time (C5). Voice policy: named human or clearly labelled narration; no AI "expert". Target 6 to 8 minutes (about 1,000 words). Items marked [CHECK] need a human check against the source before publishing. The title uses the September 12-month figure; the on-screen label must say "12 months to September 2026".

## 1. Hook (0:00 to 0:35)
Airline fares in the US consumer price index are up 22.4 percent over twelve months to September. [chart: BLS airline fares, 12-month change, Sep 2026; all items for comparison, 3.7 percent]

For calendar 2026, the world's airlines, as a group, are forecast to earn $4.50 of profit on each passenger they carry. That is a forecast for the year, not the same twelve months. [chart: IATA, net profit per passenger, 2025 estimate $9.10, 2026 forecast $4.50]

Prices up by a fifth, profit per passenger cut roughly in half. Both numbers come from official sources. They are measuring different things, so we will take them one at a time.

## 2. What the price number is (0:35 to 2:00)
The 22.4 percent comes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It is the airline fares part of the Consumer Price Index, and it is a sample of fares paid by US consumers, not every ticket sold. [on screen: BLS release, Table 1, row "Airline fares"]

The index was 258.0 in September 2025 and 315.9 in September 2026. In the October release, published on 10 November, it stood at 327.8. That is a further rise of 3.8 percent before seasonal adjustment, or 0.4 percent after it. [CHECK: BLS has not printed an October 12-month rate, so do not say "22 percent in October"]

Note the scale. All items rose 3.7 percent in the year to September. Airfares rose six times as fast. [chart: airline fares vs all items vs energy, 20.4 percent]

Energy is the clue. Gasoline in the same release is up 34.2 percent over the year. Fuel is the common thread, so look at who buys it in bulk.

## 3. What the profit number is (2:00 to 4:00)
The $4.50 is from IATA, the airline trade association. In its June 2026 outlook it forecast that airlines worldwide will make a net profit of $23.0 billion this year. Its estimate for 2025 was $45 billion. [on screen: IATA press release No. 26, 7 June 2026]

Divide by passengers and you get $4.50 per passenger in 2026, against $9.10 in 2025. The net margin falls from 4.2 percent to 2.0 percent. Load factor, the share of seats filled, is forecast at 84.0 percent, up from 83.5 percent.

Two limits. First, this is a worldwide forecast, not US airlines and not any one ticket. Second, it is a forecast from a trade body, not audited results.

## 4. Where the squeeze comes from (4:00 to 5:30)
IATA's own numbers show the pressure. [chart: IATA fuel bill $252 billion in 2025 to $350 billion forecast for 2026]

The fuel bill is forecast at $350 billion, up from $252 billion. That is $98 billion more, our subtraction of two IATA figures. Jet fuel is assumed to average $152 a barrel, against $90 the year before. Fuel is 31.4 percent of airline costs in the forecast, and about a third of the fuel is hedged. [CHECK: re-read the IATA table for the hedge wording]

Operating profit is forecast at $48.0 billion, down from $76.4 billion. Revenue is forecast at $1.165 trillion.

Put these together. Fares rose in the US consumer data, the industry fuel bill is forecast to rise by $98 billion, and profit per passenger is forecast to shrink. That is consistent with a fuel shock, but the data here does not prove how much of the fare rise is fuel, and it cannot split one ticket into slices. We did not find a source that does that for a single US ticket.

## 5. What we cannot say (5:30 to 6:40)
We cannot say what your last ticket cost because of fuel. The BLS index is a sample and its own notes say it measures change, not a price. We cannot say US airlines earn $4.50, because IATA's figure is global. And we cannot say the forecast will come true; IATA revises its forecasts, as this one shows. The December 2025 forecast for 2026 was $41 billion, and it was cut to $23.0 billion in June. [CHECK: confirm $41 billion wording against the IATA June release]

What the data does support is narrow: fares in the US index rose sharply; the industry forecast says profit per passenger fell; and the fuel bill is the cost line that the forecast shows rising by the most we could quantify.

## 6. Close (6:40 to 7:15)
If a headline gives you a price rise and a profit figure, ask which people the number covers, whether it is a sample or a total, and whether it is a result or a forecast.

This is not financial advice. It describes how two published numbers are built. Sources are in the description, and every figure on screen has its source tag.
