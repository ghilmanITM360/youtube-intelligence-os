## 0:00 — Cold open `[SIMULATION]` `ESTIMATE`

*Black frame. A pinpoint of white light, bullet-sized, sits in empty space. HUD: STATUS RELEASED.*

In a fraction of a second, this tiny thing releases about as much energy as the entire Sun gives off in a whole second.

Not just the sunlight that reaches Earth. Everything the Sun puts out, in every direction.

Then it's gone.

*Hard cut to a calm, ordinary-looking grey pellet resting on a table. HUD: STATUS CONTAINED.*

This is how it started: something about the size of a handgun bullet. It's made of the material inside a neutron star.

So what would one actually be like?

*Title card: What If a Bullet Were Made of Neutron Star?*

## 0:35 — What we're holding `[REAL DATA]`

*Original illustration: a giant star, its core collapsing, a flash, and a small bright ball left behind. Scale bar: "~25 km".*

When a very massive star runs out of fuel, its core can collapse in an instant, and the outside blows off as a supernova.

What's sometimes left is a neutron star: typically around one and a half times the mass of our Sun, squeezed into a ball roughly twenty-five kilometres across. That's about the size of a city.

NASA's NICER telescope, on the Space Station, measured one of these, a pulsar called J0030, at about twenty-five kilometres across.

Inside, atoms have been crushed out of existence. What's left is mostly neutrons, packed shoulder to shoulder.

## 1:20 — One teaspoon `[THE MATH]`

*A teaspoon outline. HUD MASS readout spins up.*

How dense is that? Take one teaspoon.

Depending on how deep inside the star you scoop it, that teaspoon weighs somewhere between one and five billion tonnes. That's the same as half a cubic kilometre to almost two cubic kilometres of solid rock.

A hill, in a spoon.

*The whole Earth shrinks in a slow zoom until it is a grey ball next to a football stadium outline.*

Or try it the other way around. Squeeze the entire Earth to this density: every ocean, every mountain, the whole iron core.

It would fit in a ball between about two hundred and twenty-five and three hundred and fifty metres across. That's the whole planet, in a ball you could walk around in about ten minutes.

## 1:50 — The bullet `[THE MATH]`

*The spoon becomes a bullet-sized pellet. Beside it, a cube of rock grows until a balance between them sits level. Labels: "0.7 cm³" and "412 m".*

Now our bullet. A normal handgun bullet takes up about seven tenths of a cubic centimetre, much less than a teaspoon.

Fill that volume with neutron-star material, and it weighs at least a hundred and ninety million tonnes. From the densest part of the star, it's over seven hundred million.

Even at the low end, it balances a cube of rock four hundred metres on each side.

For the next few minutes, let's pretend something holds it together. Call it a magic container. We'll come back to what happens without one.

## 2:35 — Its own gravity `[THE MATH]`

*HUD: PULL AT 1 m → 1.3–4.8 g. A coffee cup on the table slides toward the pellet.*

Something this heavy has noticeable gravity of its own.

Stand one metre away, and it pulls you sideways harder than the whole Earth pulls you down: between one point three and almost five times Earth's gravity, depending on the density.

You wouldn't stand next to it. You'd fall into it.

*A single dust grain circles the pellet on a 1 m loop. Readout: "ORBIT: 13–25 km/h · 1–2 s per lap".*

In empty space, away from Earth, a grain of dust could orbit it. One metre out, the grain would circle at about jogging-to-cycling speed, thirteen to twenty-five kilometres an hour, and go all the way round every one to two seconds.

A tiny solar system, with a bullet for a sun.

Right at its surface, the pull is tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand times Earth's gravity. Anything that touches it is pressed flat onto it.

## 3:15 — Firing it `[THE MATH]`

*A generic, featureless launcher outline beside the pellet. No real firearm design. Arrow labelled "same push".*

So what if you tried to fire it?

Give it the same push that launches a normal bullet at roughly the speed of sound.

A normal bullet weighs about eight grams. Ours weighs at least a hundred and ninety million tonnes. The same push gets it moving at about a hundred-billionth of a metre per second.

In a year, it would travel less than half a millimetre.

*The launcher tips over and slides onto the pellet, crumpling against it.*

And that's if the launcher survived. The pellet's gravity pulls the launcher onto it instead. It doesn't leave the gun. The gun joins it.

## 4:00 — Dropping it `[SIMULATION]` `[EXCERPT → Day 4 Short starts]`

*A label stays on screen: "ASSUMPTION: something holds it together". The pellet rests on a wooden floor.*

All right. We can't fire it. What if we just put it down?

Here's the problem. All that weight presses on an area about the size of a fingernail.

The pressure underneath is about a hundred million times more than solid granite can take.

*The floor gives way like water around a stone. The pellet sinks through floorboards, then concrete, then bedrock.*

So it doesn't sit on the floor. It sinks through it. Then through the concrete, then through the rock below, the way a pebble sinks through water.

Nothing on Earth is strong enough to hold it up, so it keeps going down, toward the centre of the planet.

You can't put it down. You can only let go of it. `[EXCERPT ends]`

## 5:00 — Removing the magic `[THE MATH]` `ESTIMATE`

*The "ASSUMPTION" label fades out. HUD: STATUS CONTAINED → RELEASED.*

Now the real question. We've been pretending something holds it together. Nothing does.

Inside a neutron star, this material stays packed because the whole star's gravity is crushing it from every side. Take a bullet-sized piece out, and that crushing goes away.

So it springs apart.

Each neutron in there is carrying stored-up energy from being squeezed. Add it up for every neutron in our bullet, and you get around three times ten to the twenty-six joules. From the densest material, it's closer to ten to the twenty-seven.

That's roughly one to three seconds of the Sun's entire output, from something you could have held between two fingers.

*Label: ESTIMATE. ORDER OF MAGNITUDE.*

That figure is an estimate. The physics of matter this dense is still being worked out, so treat the first digit as rough. The scale isn't in doubt.

## 6:05 — What's left `[REAL DATA]`

*An expanding cloud of dots; a clock labelled "~15 min".*

And the neutrons that fly out don't last either.

A free neutron, out on its own, doesn't survive: on average, it lasts about fifteen minutes before decaying into a proton, an electron and an almost undetectable particle called an antineutrino.

That adds a little more energy, a few percent on top. Then our bullet is gone, turned into a hot, spreading cloud of ordinary hydrogen.

## 6:35 — The answer `[THE MATH]`

*The HUD shows all three readouts side by side: MASS 190–710 MILLION t · PULL AT 1 m 1.3–4.8 g · RELEASE ≈ 1–3 s OF SUNLIGHT.*

So, what if a bullet were made of neutron star?

If something magic held it together, it would weigh as much as a small hill. It would pull you in from a metre away. It couldn't be fired, and it couldn't be put down: it would sink straight into the Earth.

And without the magic, it couldn't exist at all, outside a neutron star.

The only place a neutron-star bullet is safe is inside a neutron star.

*Echo: the pinpoint of light from the cold open, now labelled with the numbers.*
