## 0:00 — Cold open `[SIMULATION]` `ESTIMATE`

*Black frame. A pinpoint of white light, bullet-sized, sits in empty space. HUD: STATUS RELEASED.*

In a fraction of a second, this tiny thing releases about as much energy as the entire Sun gives off in a whole second.

Not just the sunlight that reaches Earth. Everything the Sun puts out, in every direction.

Then it's gone.

*Hard cut to a calm, ordinary-looking grey pellet resting on a table. HUD: STATUS CONTAINED.*

This is how it started: something about the size of a handgun bullet, made of the material inside a neutron star.

So what would one actually be like?

*Title card: What If a Bullet Were Made of Neutron Star?*


## 0:26 — What we're holding `[REAL DATA]`

*Original illustration: a giant star, its core collapsing, a flash, and a small bright ball left behind. Scale bar: "~25 km".*

When a very massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses in an instant, and the outside blows off as a supernova.

What's sometimes left is a neutron star: around one and a half times the mass of our Sun, squeezed into a ball roughly twenty-five kilometres across. A city, with the mass of a star.

Inside, atoms have been crushed out of existence. What's left is mostly neutrons, packed shoulder to shoulder.


## 0:54 — One teaspoon `[THE MATH]`

*A teaspoon outline. HUD MASS readout spins up.*

How dense is that? Take one teaspoon.

Depending on how deep inside the star you scoop it, that teaspoon weighs between one and five billion tonnes. That's half a cubic kilometre to almost two cubic kilometres of solid rock.

A hill, in a spoon. Please don't stir.


## 1:12 — The bullet `[THE MATH]`

*The spoon becomes a bullet-sized pellet. Beside it, a cube of rock grows until a balance between them sits level. Labels: "0.7 cm³" and "412 m".*

Now our bullet. A handgun bullet takes up about seven tenths of a cubic centimetre, much less than a teaspoon.

Fill that volume with neutron-star material, and it weighs at least a hundred and ninety million tonnes. It balances a cube of rock four hundred metres on each side.

For the next minute, let's pretend something holds it together. Call it a magic container. We'll come back to what happens without one.


## 1:39 — Its own gravity `[THE MATH]`

*HUD: PULL AT 1 m → 1.3–4.8 g. A coffee cup on the table slides toward the pellet.*

Something this heavy has gravity of its own.

Stand one metre away, and it pulls you sideways harder than the whole Earth pulls you down: between one point three and almost five times Earth's gravity, depending on the density.

You wouldn't stand next to it. You'd fall into it.


## 1:58 — Firing it `[THE MATH]`

*A generic, featureless launcher outline beside the pellet. No real firearm design. Arrow labelled "same push".*

So what if you tried to fire it?

Give it the same push that launches a normal bullet at roughly the speed of sound. A normal bullet weighs about eight grams. Ours weighs at least a hundred and ninety million tonnes, so the same push gets it moving at about a hundred-billionth of a metre per second. In a year, it would travel less than half a millimetre.

*The launcher tips over and slides onto the pellet, crumpling against it.*

And that's if the launcher survived. The pellet's gravity pulls the launcher onto it instead. It doesn't leave the gun. The gun joins it.


## 2:32 — Dropping it `[SIMULATION]` `[EXCERPT → Day 4 Short starts]`

*A label stays on screen: "ASSUMPTION: something holds it together". The pellet rests on a wooden floor.*

All right. We can't fire it. What if we just put it down?

All that weight presses on an area about the size of a fingernail. The pressure underneath is about a hundred million times more than solid granite can take.

*The floor gives way like water around a stone. The pellet sinks through floorboards, then concrete, then bedrock.*

So it doesn't sit on the floor. It sinks through it, then through the concrete, then through the rock below, the way a pebble sinks through water. Nothing on Earth is strong enough to hold it up, so it keeps going down, toward the centre of the planet.

You can't put it down. You can only let go of it. `[EXCERPT ends]`


## 3:09 — Removing the magic `[THE MATH]` `ESTIMATE`

*The "ASSUMPTION" label fades out. HUD: STATUS CONTAINED → RELEASED.*

Now the real question. We've been pretending something holds it together. Nothing does.

Inside a neutron star, this material stays packed because the whole star's gravity is crushing it from every side. Take a bullet-sized piece out, and that crushing goes away. So it springs apart.

Every neutron in there is carrying energy from being squeezed. Add it up and you get around three times ten to the twenty-six joules, closer to ten to the twenty-seven from the densest material. That's roughly one to three seconds of the Sun's entire output.

*Label: ESTIMATE. ORDER OF MAGNITUDE.*

That's an estimate. The physics of matter this dense is still being worked out, so treat the first digit as rough. The scale isn't in doubt.

*An expanding cloud of dots; a clock labelled "~15 min". Label: REAL DATA.*

And the loose neutrons don't last either. A free neutron survives about fifteen minutes on average before it decays. Then our bullet is gone, turned into a hot, spreading cloud of ordinary hydrogen.


## 4:02 — The answer `[THE MATH]`

*The HUD shows all three readouts side by side: MASS 190–710 MILLION t · PULL AT 1 m 1.3–4.8 g · RELEASE ≈ 1–3 s OF SUNLIGHT.*

So, what if a bullet were made of neutron star?

If something magic held it together, it would weigh as much as a small hill, and pull you in from a metre away. It couldn't be fired, and it couldn't be put down: it would sink straight into the Earth. And without the magic, it couldn't exist at all, outside a neutron star.

The only place a neutron-star bullet is safe is inside a neutron star.

*Echo: the pinpoint of light from the cold open, now labelled with the numbers.*
