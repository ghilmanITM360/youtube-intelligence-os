## 0:00 — Cold open `[SIMULATION]`

*Tracker: ALTITUDE 400 km · SPEED 0 · G 0. A suited figure hangs in black space above a thin blue limb. No capsule, no rocket.*

Four hundred kilometres up. No rocket. No capsule. No sideways speed. Just you, standing still on nothing.

For four minutes, nothing touches you. There's almost no air up here to push back, so you just keep getting faster.

By the time you reach the top of the atmosphere, you're doing more than eight thousand kilometres an hour.

Then the air arrives. All at once.

*Cut to black.*

That's the top rung. To understand why it goes so badly up there, we have to start at the bottom.

*Title card.*

What happens if you fall from every height, from one metre all the way to the edge of space?

## 0:40 — Ground rules `[THE MATH]`

*The ruler draws itself, 1 m at the bottom, 400 km at the top. Three rule cards stack beside it.*

Some quick rules, so the numbers mean something.

- You start completely still.
- You fall belly-down, like a skydiver.
- Above about nineteen kilometres you wear a pressure suit; we'll get to why.

Every speed comes from one simple model of a body falling through Earth's atmosphere. We tested that model against a real jump from the edge of space, which you'll see later. Where a number is an estimate, the screen says so.

## 1:05 — Rung 1: one metre `[THE MATH]`

*Tracker: 1 m. A figure steps off a kitchen counter.*

One metre. A little higher than a kitchen counter.

You're in the air for under half a second, and you reach the floor at about sixteen kilometres an hour. That's not enough time to finish saying "whoa".

## 1:20 — Rung 2: ten metres `[THE MATH]`

*Tracker: 10 m. A three-storey building outline.*

Ten metres, roughly a three-storey building.

Just under a second and a half, and you arrive at fifty kilometres an hour: town driving speed.

The air has barely noticed you yet. With no air at all, you'd land at almost exactly the same speed.

## 1:40 — Rung 3: one hundred metres `[THE MATH]`

*Tracker: 100 m. Two speed bars side by side: "NO AIR 159" and "WITH AIR 138".*

A hundred metres. Now the air starts to matter.

In a vacuum, you'd hit at about a hundred and sixty kilometres an hour. With the air pushing back, it's closer to a hundred and thirty-eight.

That gap is the first sign of the most important number in this video.

## 2:00 — Rung 4: one kilometre, terminal velocity `[THE MATH]`

*Tracker: 1 km. The SPEED readout climbs, then freezes at ~200. Two arrows, WEIGHT down and AIR up, grow until they match.*

As you speed up, the air pushes harder. At around two hundred kilometres an hour, that push equals your weight, and you stop speeding up. That's terminal velocity.

You get to about ninety-five percent of it in seven seconds, after falling roughly two hundred and twenty metres.

Which leads to something odd. Drop from one kilometre, or three, or five, and you hit the ground at roughly the same two hundred kilometres an hour.

Down here, past this rung, extra height buys you more time in the air. It doesn't buy you a harder landing.

At least, not down here.

## 2:40 — Rung 5: ten kilometres `[THE MATH]` `[EXCERPT → Day 2 Short starts]`

*Tracker: 10 km. Air-density dots thin out toward the top of the frame. A thermometer chip reads −50 °C.*

Ten kilometres. The air here is around minus fifty degrees, and much thinner. Thin air pushes back less, so up here your top speed is higher.

You peak at about three hundred and twenty kilometres an hour, roughly twenty seconds after you step off.

And then something strange happens. You slow down.

*The SPEED readout peaks, then falls while the figure keeps dropping: 320 → 265 → 200.*

As you drop into thicker air, it pushes harder and brakes you. By five kilometres up you're down to about two hundred and sixty-five. Near the ground, you're back to roughly two hundred.

That's the pattern for every jump from here on. The higher you start, the faster your peak, and the more of that speed the air takes back before you land. `[EXCERPT ends]`

## 3:25 — Rung 6: nineteen kilometres, the pressure line `[THE MATH]`

*Tracker: 19 km. A gauge needle drops to "6.5 kPa" beside a "6.3 kPa: water boils at 37 °C" mark.*

Around nineteen kilometres, the air pressure falls to roughly the point where water boils at body temperature.

Your blood stays under pressure because your heart keeps it there. The moisture in your mouth and lungs doesn't get that protection.

So from this rung up, the pressure suit isn't optional.

## 3:45 — Rung 7: thirty-nine kilometres, the real record `[REAL DATA]`

*Tracker: 39 km. The label switches to REAL DATA. A date chip: 14 OCT 2012. Original illustration of a suited figure on a small platform. No real footage.*

Thirty-nine kilometres. This one isn't a simulation.

On the fourteenth of October 2012, Felix Baumgartner stepped off a capsule at thirty-eight thousand, nine hundred and sixty-nine metres. The air there is roughly two hundred and sixty times thinner than at the ground.

About fifty seconds into the fall, close to twenty-eight kilometres up, he reached one thousand, three hundred and fifty-seven point six kilometres an hour. That's Mach 1.25, faster than sound.

He went into a flat spin for about thirteen seconds, got out of it, and spent around four minutes and twenty seconds in freefall before opening his parachute.

*Split panel. REAL: 27.8 km, 50 s. MODEL: 27.5 km, 51 s. Label: THE MATH.*

This is the check we promised. Our model puts his peak at twenty-seven and a half kilometres after fifty-one seconds, within about a second of the real thing.

It isn't perfect. It keeps him faster than sound for about six seconds longer than he actually was. So treat every number above this rung as close, not exact.

## 4:55 — Rung 8: forty-one kilometres `[REAL DATA]`

*Tracker: 41.4 km. Date chip: 24 OCT 2014. A small drogue parachute trails behind the figure.*

Two years later, Alan Eustace jumped from even higher: forty-one thousand, four hundred and twenty-two metres.

He fell with a small stabilising parachute called a drogue, so the speed record stayed with Baumgartner.

A higher start, a slower fall, by choice.

## 5:15 — Rung 9: sixty kilometres `[SIMULATION]`

*Tracker: 60 km. The label switches to SIMULATION and stays there for the rest of the climb.*

Nobody has jumped from here, so from now on it's the model.

Sixty kilometres. You'd peak at about two thousand, two hundred and fifty kilometres an hour, around twice the speed of sound. As you drop into thicker air, it would brake you at about two and a half g.

## 5:35 — Rung 10: one hundred kilometres, the Kármán line `[REAL DATA]` + `[SIMULATION]`

*Tracker: 100 km. A dashed line labelled "FAI: 100 km (a convention)".*

A hundred kilometres. The international body that certifies these records, the FAI, uses this line as the boundary of space.

It's a convention, not a wall. Nothing physical changes here, and not everyone draws the line in the same place.

Drop from it standing still, and you'd peak at around three thousand six hundred kilometres an hour, more than three times the speed of sound, and feel close to five g as the air slows you.

About five and a half minutes later, you reach the ground at roughly two hundred kilometres an hour. The same as from one kilometre.

## 6:15 — Rung 11: four hundred kilometres `[SIMULATION]` `ESTIMATE`

*Tracker: back to the cold-open frame, now with numbers. G readout rises to 20, with a bar showing "> 10 g for ~11 s".*

Which brings us back to the top. Four hundred kilometres, standing still.

Gravity up here is still about eighty-nine percent of what you feel right now. There's just almost nothing to fall through.

You pass a hundred kilometres doing about eight thousand four hundred kilometres an hour. Then the air hits. The deceleration peaks around twenty g, and stays above ten g for roughly eleven seconds.

The air in front of you gets squeezed and heated to thousands of degrees. That one's an estimate, and the real number depends on details our simple model leaves out.

For scale: the hardest deceleration anyone has deliberately ridden was John Stapp's rocket sled in 1954, forty-six point two g. But that lasted about a second.

Eleven seconds above ten g, in this kind of fall, is far beyond what anyone is put through on purpose, and we're not going to guess how it ends.

## 7:05 — The twist: orbit `[REAL DATA]`

*A space-station silhouette (generic, original drawing) crosses the 400 km rung, moving sideways. A dotted path curves around the Earth.*

Here's the strange part. The International Space Station flies at about this height.

It isn't up there because it's high. Things at four hundred kilometres fall. You just watched it happen.

The station stays up because it's moving sideways at about five miles, eight kilometres, every second, going all the way around the Earth roughly every ninety minutes.

It is falling the whole time. It's just moving sideways so fast that the ground keeps curving away before it can land.

That's why the title says "edge of space" and not "orbit". Falling from orbit isn't about height. It's about speed.

## 7:50 — The answer `[THE MATH]`

*The full ruler with every rung lit. Three bracket labels appear one at a time.*

So, falling from every height:

- Below about a kilometre, the height decides how hard you land.
- From a kilometre up, the air takes over, and you land at around two hundred kilometres an hour no matter where you started.
- Start higher than about thirty-five kilometres, and you can break the sound barrier. Then the thick air near the ground takes all of it back.

The higher you fall, the faster you go, and the more the atmosphere has to slow you down.
