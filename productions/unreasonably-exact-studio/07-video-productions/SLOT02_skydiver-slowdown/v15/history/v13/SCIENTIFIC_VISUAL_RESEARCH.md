## Decisions that came from this research

1. **Relative airflow comes before force (E1).** The NASA frame-equivalence statement is shown literally, as a camera move. The drag arrow appears only after the air is visibly moving up past the body. Airflow is drawn as dots with short motion trails; force is a solid teal arrow. They never share a style.
2. **Density alone (I1).** Two equal boxes; dot count ∝ model density at 10 km (0.41 kg/m³) and 600 m (1.16 kg/m³). Then two identical bodies at the **same speed** show a drag arrow about 2.8× longer in the denser air (the density ratio, speed fixed). Nothing else is on screen.
3. **Speed alone (J1).** One fixed air-sample box labelled "same air · same posture". Speed ×1→×2 and drag ×1→×4, with a dashed ghost of the ×1 arrow left behind.
4. **Force comparison, Option B chosen over the V12 ×30 lens.** This decision was made from the proofs (`qa/bars_strip.png`, `MOTION_DESIGN_PROOFS/D2_*`). The lens needed a scale badge, a leader line and a second picture inside the picture. Two bars side by side, from one baseline on one scale, show the same thing directly. The bars are 280 units tall, so the model's 3.2% excess at 27 s is about 9 units (about 27 px at 1080 width). A real camera push (×2.25) toward the bar tops makes the violet excess clearly visible. Nothing is exaggerated: both bars scale together.
5. **Opening.** I restored the V11 composition (the owner's preferred one) and added true layers: a far sky/hills layer, a near-fields layer, mid clouds and the subjects, each zooming and tilting at its own rate.
