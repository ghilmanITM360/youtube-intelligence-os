## Verified live sources, 2026-11-25

Read in Browser: [NASA falling body](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/falling-object-with-air-resistance/), [NASA drag](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/drag-equation/), [NASA metric atmosphere](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/earth-atmosphere-equation-metric/).

Weight points downward; drag resists relative motion. Drag depends on density, speed, area and shape. The standard atmosphere has lower density at greater height. Combining these relationships permits a fixed-posture falling body to enter denser air with speed above the changing local equilibrium speed. Upward drag can then exceed weight while velocity remains downward. This last conclusion is an inference from the equations, not a NASA statement about a particular person. The example calculation was rerun: see ../qa/MODEL_CROSSCHECK.json. It is not a trajectory used for film timing.

The final film uses unnumbered speed and schematic forces. No physiological claim, landing, survival guarantee, actual altitude remaining, measured speed or instructions for performing a jump are depicted. Clouds and terrain establish relative travel and context, not quantitative acceleration. Density dots and force lengths are educational graphics.
