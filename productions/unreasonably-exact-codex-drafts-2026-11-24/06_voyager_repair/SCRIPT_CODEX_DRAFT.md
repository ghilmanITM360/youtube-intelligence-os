## Voiceover

In April 2024, a team on Earth sent a repair command to Voyager 1. Then they waited almost two days to find out whether it had worked.

There was no wrench in the repair kit. The problem was inside a computer, more than 24 billion kilometres away.

Voyager 1 had launched in 1977. By November 2023 it was still receiving commands, but the science and engineering information coming back was unreadable. The team could hear a signal, yet could not read the report it was meant to carry. For months, they could not see the spacecraft's health in the usual way.

One of Voyager's onboard computers, the flight data subsystem, collects information from the spacecraft and prepares it for transmission. Think of it as the part that puts the report into an envelope before the radio sends it. The radio was not enough on its own: a healthy carrier cannot tell you what is inside a report that was never packaged correctly.

In March, the team sent a command that prompted a readout of the computer's memory. Examining it, engineers found corrupted memory. NASA reported that a chip holding part of the affected memory was not working. The cause was uncertain: possibilities included an energetic particle or age. The team could not simply reach out and replace the chip.

They could, however, change where the computer kept its instructions. The affected code was too large to fit in one available place, so engineers split it into sections and put those sections in different parts of the memory. That meant changing the code so the pieces could still work together, and adjusting references to their old locations.

The first piece they moved was the code responsible for packaging engineering data. On April eighteenth, they sent the revised instructions. At that distance, the signal needed about twenty-two and a half hours to reach Voyager. A response needed roughly the same time to come home. This is the travel time for the two legs together, before any additional work on the spacecraft or on Earth.

On April twentieth the reply arrived. The engineering data was readable again. For the first time in roughly five months, the team could check the spacecraft's health. That did not mean every part of the repair was complete. NASA said other affected sections, including those needed for science data, still had to be moved.

The team did not magically heal the damaged hardware. They made the computer work around it. That distinction matters: the fix was a careful rearrangement of instructions in the memory that remained usable.

There is a later chapter, and it needs a date. In November 2026 NASA reported a new interruption: a carrier signal was being received, but science and engineering data were again missing. Engineers were investigating. A similar symptom does not establish the same cause. Check NASA's latest update before treating that description as current.

Around the same month Voyager reached one light-day from Earth. Near that milestone, a signal takes about a day each way. The spacecraft is farther away now than it was during the 2024 repair, so the old forty-five-hour round trip and the later two-day approximation describe different moments.

How do you repair a machine that far away? First understand what its messages can and cannot tell you. Then find an instruction you can change, send it across the distance, and wait for the evidence to come back. In 2024, the answer was to move the code around the broken memory.
