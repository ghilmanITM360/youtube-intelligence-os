## Voiceover

Fire a short laser pulse at the Moon. How long before its light can get back to Earth?

About two and a half seconds, on average. The Moon's distance changes, so the actual round trip changes too.

Astronauts left arrays of special reflectors on the lunar surface. Observatories aim laser pulses at them; a tiny fraction of the light returns toward Earth. The station times the outgoing and returning light. Since the light travelled out and back, take half the travel time and multiply by the speed of light to estimate the one-way distance.

But this is not one perfect stopwatch reading from one photon. The Apache Point lunar ranging experiment combines many detected returns and applies corrections to make a measurement. Its reported millimetre-scale precision belongs to that measurement process, not to a promise that each individual pulse gives a millimetre-accurate answer.

The answer is roughly two and a half seconds for the round trip. The patience comes in turning those faint returns into a useful measurement.
