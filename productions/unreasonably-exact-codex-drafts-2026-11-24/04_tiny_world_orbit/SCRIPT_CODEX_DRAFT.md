## Voiceover

Imagine jumping sideways and watching the ground curve away beneath you. You keep falling toward it, but you never quite catch it. Could a world be small enough for a human jump to become an orbit?

We can make one on paper. It is a perfectly smooth, round ball of rock, three kilometres in radius, with a uniform density of two thousand kilograms per cubic metre. There is no air, no spin and no other body pulling on you. This is a toy world, not a description of an asteroid.

Its gravity is very weak. For a circular path just grazing the surface, the sideways speed works out to about 2.2 metres per second: roughly eight kilometres an hour. That sounds like an ordinary jog. But the tiny world's weak gravity also gives your feet very little grip. Reaching that speed on the ground is its own problem.

Let's grant you a launch at that speed and look at the path. Orbit is not a place where gravity switches off. Gravity keeps bending your motion toward the centre. With the right sideways speed, the path curves around the ball instead of straight down into it.

On our perfectly smooth sphere, a perfectly horizontal launch at just the right speed traces a circle that grazes the surface. The calculated lap takes about two hours and twenty minutes. The catch is hiding in the word grazes. The orbit has no clearance at the launch point. A surface with even a small bump, a body extending below its centre of mass, or a tiny error in direction can make contact.

A real jump usually points upward as well as sideways. If we launch upward from the surface, the ideal ellipse goes out and later meets the ground again. The rest of the mathematical ellipse would pass through the rock; a person cannot follow that part. A long hop is not automatically a complete orbit.

What if you started a little above the surface instead? At that height, a precisely horizontal launch at the local circular speed could have clearance on our toy world. But now we have supplied the height and the carefully chosen velocity. Neither comes for free from an ordinary jump.

And a faster launch changes the shape again. For a level launch from the surface, a bit above circular speed produces an elongated path returning to its starting altitude. At about the square root of two times the circular speed, the ideal escape threshold is reached: roughly 3.2 metres per second here. Escape from our isolated ball does not mean escape from a real solar system.

Could a person actually deliver those velocities under such weak gravity? That is a separate biomechanics problem. We have assumed the launch; we have not measured a person jumping on this world. Real small bodies also have irregular shapes and spin, and neighbouring bodies pull on them.

So can a sideways jump become an orbit? In the toy calculation, the necessary speed is human-sized, but a surface launch has a clearance problem. Give the jumper the right height and an almost perfectly sideways velocity, and the path can circle the world. The difficult part is not finding a place without gravity. It is arranging for the ground to stay out of the way.
