## Voiceover

Two identical fallers start above the same patch of ground. One begins one kilometre up. The other starts a hundred kilometres up, at a commonly used boundary of space. They let go at the same moment. Which one reaches the ground faster?

There are two ways to hear that question. Who arrives first? And who has more speed at the moment of impact? The answers are not the same.

To compare them, we need a make-believe experiment. Each starts from rest, falls vertically, holds the same fixed shape and has no parachute. There is no wind. We give them identical suits and let a simple atmosphere and gravity act on them. This describes a calculation, not a real person's trajectory. It does not predict survival.

The lower faller meets thick air almost immediately. At first gravity wins: speed rises. But moving through air creates an opposing force. As the faller gets faster, that force gets stronger. Near the ground, the two forces become nearly balanced. In one version of our model, the impact speed is about 190 kilometres an hour and the fall takes about twenty-two seconds.

The other faller has a very different trip. At a hundred kilometres the air is extremely thin. There is little drag at first, so gravity has time to build a much larger speed. In this same model the speed peaks at roughly 3,600 kilometres an hour, high above the ground. That is the highest reading on the speedometer, not the impact reading.

Then the atmosphere thickens around the faller. The drag force in the calculation grows until it exceeds the faller's weight. The faller is still going down, but speed begins to drop. The peak and the landing are separate events.

After about six minutes, the model reaches the ground at roughly 190 kilometres an hour: close to its result for the one-kilometre fall. So the lower faller arrives first, while the calculated impact speeds are similar. More starting height has made an enormous difference to the trip and little difference to this particular model's final speed.

Why not say they are exactly the same? Because the answer depends on the model. Turn a body head down and its area facing the air changes. Change the suit, posture or weather, and the speed changes too. And our calculation holds one drag parameter fixed even as the flow passes through very different regimes, including speeds several times the speed of sound. It leaves out heating and changing body position. The exact high-altitude peak is especially uncertain.

There is a real-world reference, though it is not a test of our hundred-kilometre fall. In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from almost 39 kilometres and reached about 1,358 kilometres an hour during the descent. That was an observed flight, with its own equipment and changing posture. It is not the model's peak and it was not a jump from space.

There is another distinction at the top of our picture. Our faller starts with no sideways speed. A spacecraft returning from orbit arrives with a great deal of sideways motion; simply being at the same altitude does not make its path the same.

So, in this thought experiment, the fall from space takes much longer and reaches a much higher peak. In the simple fixed-shape model, the two impact speeds are similar. A speedometer at the middle of the journey and a speedometer at the end are answering different questions.
