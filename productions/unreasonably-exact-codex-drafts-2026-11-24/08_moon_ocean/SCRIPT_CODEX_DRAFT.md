## Voiceover

One night the Moon disappears. No explosion, no falling pieces. We will simply remove it and its gravitational influence, leaving Earth, its ocean and its motion otherwise as they were at that instant. What happens to the sea?

It does not fly away. Earth's gravity still holds the ocean. But the Moon was doing something subtler: its gravity pulls a little more strongly on the near side of Earth than on the far side. That difference helps drive the long, moving waves we experience as tides.

A useful first picture is a planet covered by an uninterrupted ocean. In that simplified picture, the Moon's differential pull produces a stretching pattern, with a bulge toward the Moon and another on the far side. The second is not water being pulled away from Earth. It appears when we compare the weaker lunar pull there with the pull on Earth's centre.

Our actual ocean does not have an uninterrupted route around the planet. Continents interrupt it; ocean depth changes; rotation and friction matter. Tidal waves travel through basins and meet coastlines. The time and height of high water depend on the place. Some coasts have two highs in a lunar day, some have one, and some have a mixture. The neat pair of bulges is a starting picture, not a timetable for every beach.

Now take away the Moon's pull. Does the water instantly settle into its new pattern? No. At our chosen instant the ocean already has moving water and a changing surface. Removing a force does not erase that motion. Existing waves and currents would continue and change as the ocean responds; friction would dissipate energy. How that transition looks, and how long particular motions persist, requires an ocean model. We cannot put a reliable clock on it from this thought experiment alone.

Another tide-maker is still in the sky: the Sun. It pulls very strongly on Earth, but it is so far away that the difference between its pull on the near and far sides is comparatively small. That difference still drives solar tides.

Today the lunar and solar effects combine. Around new and full Moon their tide-generating effects reinforce each other in a simplified picture; around the quarter Moons they partly oppose each other. The familiar spring-and-neap cycle comes from their changing alignment. Remove the Moon, and that particular lunar-solar cycle loses one of its two participants.

But do not turn the relative strength of those forces into a percentage of water left at every coast. A harbour is not a force meter. Its shape, depth and connection to the ocean affect how it responds at each tidal frequency. Even now, local geography can delay high water and amplify or reduce the range. A moonless ocean would still have solar forcing, but predicting a specific harbour's new water levels needs an appropriate ocean model and a physical oceanographer.

Wind, storms and other non-lunar influences would continue to move water too. Our scenario does not settle the long-term questions about Earth's rotation, tilt or climate, so we are keeping those outside this ocean experiment.

The answer is that the sea stays on Earth, existing motion continues through a transition, and the Sun continues to drive tides. What the new tide looks like on your shore is a local, dynamic question, not a universal fraction of today's tide.
