## 1. Hook (0:00 to 0:45)
Here is a sentence from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, in its October 2026 consumer price release. Quote: BLS cannot publish 12-month percent changes for October 2026 for indexes where October 2025 data are unavailable. [on screen: bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm, Oct 2026 release, Nov 10; CHECK: re-read the sentence verbatim]

The reason is on the same page. BLS did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of a lapse in appropriations. So the number most people mean when they say inflation, the change from a year ago, was missing for most of the basket.

This video is about what that basket contains, how much each part weighs, and what the October report could and could not say.

## 2. What the CPI is, in one minute (0:45 to 1:45)
The Consumer Price Index measures the change over time in prices paid by consumers. [on screen: BLS technical note, Brief Explanation of the CPI]

The headline index, CPI-U, covers all urban consumers, which BLS says is over 90 percent of the US population. It leaves out people in rural nonmetropolitan areas, farm families, the Armed Forces, and people in institutions.

Prices are collected every month in 75 urban areas, from about 6,000 housing units and about 22,000 retail establishments. [CHECK: counts as stated in the current technical note] Price changes are combined using weights, which represent how much of household spending each item takes. The weights are the whole story of this video, so we start there.

## 3. The basket, by weight (1:45 to 4:15)
BLS publishes the relative importance of each component: its share of the all-items index. These are the December 2025 figures, which use 2024 spending weights. [on screen: chart, one horizontal bar per group; source: bls.gov/cpi/tables/relative-importance/2025.htm; CHECK: confirm the table is still the latest edition]

Housing: 44.5 percent. Transportation: 16.3. Food and beverages: 14.5. Medical care: 8.4. Education and communication: 5.8. Recreation: 5.1. Other goods and services: 2.9. Apparel: 2.4. Together, 100.

Look inside housing. Shelter is 35.6 percent of the whole index. Inside shelter, owners' equivalent rent of residences is 26.2 percent, and rent of primary residence is 7.8 percent. [on screen: nested bar] Owners' equivalent rent is a measure of what homeowners would pay to rent their own home. It is a larger weight than all of food and beverages put together at the group level, and it does not involve a purchase.

Now the pieces people feel at the register. Food at home: 8.3 percent. Food away from home: 5.4. Gasoline: 2.9. Electricity: 2.5. Motor vehicle insurance: 2.8. New vehicles: 3.8. Used cars and trucks: 2.8. Airline fares: 0.9. [on screen: bar chart sorted by weight; every value from the table above]

One consequence, stated as arithmetic and not as an opinion: a 10 percent move in airline fares, weighing 0.9 percent, changes the all-items index by about 0.09 percentage points. The same 10 percent in owners' equivalent rent changes it by about 2.6. That is why the basket matters more than any single headline item. [CHECK: arithmetic is weight times change, ignoring interaction effects]

## 4. What the October report said (4:15 to 6:15)
All items rose 0.4 percent in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, after rising 0.6 percent in September. [on screen: table, BLS Table A]

Shelter rose 0.3 percent and, in BLS's words, accounted for over thirty percent of the monthly increase. Inside it, owners' equivalent rent rose 0.3 percent and rent rose 0.2 percent. That follows from the weights above: a modest move in a very heavy item.

Food rose 0.5 percent. Food at home rose 0.4 and food away from home 0.5. Energy rose 0.7 percent after rising 5.2 percent in September. Gasoline rose 1.0 percent seasonally adjusted. Before seasonal adjustment, BLS says gasoline fell 0.7 percent. [on screen: both numbers side by side; CHECK both against the release]

All items less food and energy, the core measure, rose 0.3 percent, as it did in September and August. Used cars and trucks fell 1.2 percent. Motor vehicle insurance rose 0.7.

Two different things are being measured here: the monthly change, seasonally adjusted, and the change from a year earlier, unadjusted. October had the first. For most items it could not have the second. The table shows a 12-month figure only where October 2025 data exist: for example gasoline, 37.4 percent, used cars and trucks, minus 3.3, new vehicles, 0.7. [CHECK: re-read all three in Table A; do not quote any 12-month figure for an item that shows a dash]

## 5. Why a missing month matters (6:15 to 7:30)
A yearly change compares this month to the same month one year earlier. If one of those months was never collected, there is no clean comparison for most indexes, so BLS does not publish one. It points readers to its page on how it handles missing data. [on screen: bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/approximating-missing-data.htm; CHECK: summarise only what this page says, nothing more]

There is also a size limit built in. BLS publishes the sampling error of the index. For the all-items monthly change the standard error is 0.04 percent, so about 95 percent of such estimates fall within 0.08 percent of the figure from a complete count. A 0.4 reading is therefore, on that measure, roughly 0.32 to 0.48. [CHECK: standard error figure in the current release; this is sampling error only]

## 7. What this does and does not tell you (8:15 to 8:45)
Next time a headline says prices rose, check three things on the BLS table itself. Does it say monthly or yearly? Does it say all items, or one slice, and what share of the basket is that slice? Is the number seasonally adjusted, or not?

This is a reading guide for one published index. It does not forecast the next release, it does not say what the Federal Reserve will decide, and it is not money advice. Each figure above links to its page.

## What we did not check
We did not verify any private estimate of inflation, any survey of what households feel, or any claim about causes. We did not test whether the weights fit any one household. The weights describe an average of urban spending, not a person.
