## Part A — For Dr Rehan: How to Talk to Camera Without a Script

### A.1 The idea in one line
**Talk to the camera the way you talk to one patient in your clinic.** Do not lecture to a hall. One person, sitting across your desk, aged 65, a little worried.

The most successful real doctor in this niche, Better Health While Aging (a geriatrician, 3.5M views on one video), does not read a script. She speaks from notes, tells her own patient examples, names the guideline and recaps. In Codex's sample of 48 competitor videos, 11 were conversational like this, and real clinicians reached 119K to 4.1M views.

### A.2 The "Clinic Story" structure (8 beats, ~8–12 minutes)
Each card in Part B follows these 8 beats. Say them **in your own words**. Only the lines marked **SAY AS WRITTEN** must be exact; they are the safety lines.

| # | Beat | Time | What you do | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Scene** | 0:00–0:20 | One everyday moment the viewer has lived. Use "you". | They recognize themselves and stay |
| 2 | **Me + promise** | 0:20–0:45 | "I'm Dr Rehan, a cardiologist. By the end you'll know ___." | Trust, plus a reason to stay |
| 3 | **Patient story (start only)** | 0:45–2:30 | A real patient, details changed. **Stop before the ending.** | The open question keeps people watching |
| 4 | **Simple explanation** | 2:30–4:00 | How it works, with one everyday comparison (on each card). | Seniors remember pictures, not terms |
| 5 | **3 points** | 4:00–8:30 | Number them out loud. For each: what it is, why it matters, what to do. | A clear spine |
| 6 | **When it's an emergency** | 8:30–9:30 | Red flags and the **SAY AS WRITTEN** line. | Safety |
| 7 | **Story ending** | 9:30–10:15 | What you found and what changed. | Pays off the open question |
| 8 | **3 questions + next video** | 10:15–11:00 | Three questions to take to the doctor, then **one** specific next video. | The viewer leaves with an action |

**Exception: emergency topics (B5 heart attack, B6 mini stroke).** Here the emergency action comes **right after the scene**, in beat 1. Explanation follows. Nobody should have to wait 8 minutes to hear "call now".

### A.3 Your patient story: rules
- **Real cases only.** Never invent a patient. Before recording, pick one patient who fits the card's prompt.
- Change the name, age by a few years and any identifying detail. Say once: *"I've changed the details to protect his privacy."*
- Shape: **who they were** → **what they thought it was** → **what we found** → (beat 7) **what changed**.
- No real case fits? Say *"Let me give you a simple example"* and make it clearly hypothetical. Never present a made-up case as real.

### A.4 Words to swap
Your current videos start like: *"Cardiovascular diseases are the number one cause of mortality across the globe…"* Every viewer has heard that. Start with the **scene** instead.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| Diaphoresis | Cold sweat |
| Acute coronary syndrome | Heart attack |
| Dyspnea on exertion | Getting out of breath when you walk |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Blood pressure that drops when you stand up |
| Claudication | Calf pain when you walk that goes away when you stop |
| Postprandial glucose | Your sugar after a meal |
| Mortality | Risk of dying |
| "One should be aware…" | "You need to know…" |
| "Hi, today I'm going to discuss a very important topic" | Start with the scene |

### A.5 How to record (pick whichever feels natural)
- **Option 1, solo:** only the card's bullets on a phone or teleprompter at eye level. Record **one beat at a time**; the editor joins the best takes.
- **Option 2, interview (easiest for most doctors):** a team member beside the camera asks the **questions in brackets** on each card. You answer to the camera. The editor removes the questions.

Other tips:
- Camera at eye level, about 1 metre away.
- Speak a little slower than in clinic, and pause between beats.
- One prop per video, **shown in the first minute** (Codex: the top videos show the thing early).
- Say numbers twice, the second time in plain words.

### A.6 Rules for every video
- **SAY AS WRITTEN (medicines):** *"Please don't stop or change any medicine on your own. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist first."*
- **SAY AS WRITTEN (emergencies):** *"If this is happening now, call your emergency number, 911 in the US. Don't drive yourself."*
- Name the source on screen when you give a number.
- Never say "cure", "reverse", "guarantee", "instantly", or "this pill causes heart attacks".
- End with a short disclaimer, and put the sources in the description (the links are in [08 §B](08-CODEX-SCRIPT-RESEARCH-RESULTS.md)).
- Shorts: no "tap the link". Use YouTube's "related video" feature.

### A.7 Thumbnail system (same family on every video)
- **Rules:** a big number or a 2–4 word question; the answer hidden; a medical render or a clear object; your real photo on the right; no warning icons, no pill close-ups, no clutter.
- **Your photo:** your real clinic photo, calm, looking at the camera. **Never** an AI-generated face. AI makes only the background; add your photo and the text in Canva/Photoshop.
- **Canvas:** 1280×720, text ≥80 px from the edges, bottom-right 200×60 px empty for the timestamp.
- **Colours:** navy-teal background; text yellow `#FFD400` and white; red `#E5252A` only for the one number; heavy condensed font (Anton/Bebas) with a dark outline.
- **Layout:** text left 55%, you right 40%.
- **Squint test:** at 168×94 px the number and face must still read.
- **Standard ending for every AI prompt:** *"No text, no logos, no warning symbols, no pills, low clutter. Leave the right 40% darker and empty for a person."*
- **Competitor check (08 §C):** competitors use red "STOP / DON'T ACCEPT" warnings and hidden test names. Ours stay calm, specific and readable.

- **Text:** white "AFTER DINNER", huge yellow "10 MIN?"
- **Why this works:** competitors use squat poses and muscle drawings; the plate plus shoes says "after dinner" in one look.

**Card**
1. **Scene + the answer early:** dinner is done, and the sofa is calling. Give the answer in the first minute:
   - **SAY in your words:** *"Before you sit down, try this: walk easily for about 10 minutes, at home or outside. That's what the study I'll show you tested."*
   - *(Question: "What's the simplest thing to do after dinner?")*
2. **Promise:** "By the end you'll know **why** timing matters, **what the research actually measured**, and **who must be careful**."
3. **Story prompt:** a patient with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes who walked every morning, yet their after-dinner sugars stayed high. Stop before the ending.
4. **Simple explanation:** "After a meal, sugar arrives in your blood like a delivery truck. Your muscles are the warehouse. When they move, they open the doors and pull the sugar in."
5. **Three points:**
   - **1. Timing matters** (F1, F2). Explain **what each study measured**:
     - **Study 1:** 41 adults with type 2 diabetes, average age 60, wearing sugar monitors. Ten minutes after each meal vs 30 minutes once a day. Sugar in the 3 hours after meals was about **12% lower** with the after-meal walks. After **dinner** the difference was bigger, about **22%**.
     - **Study 2:** 10 older adults (average 69) whose sugar was starting to rise. Three 15-minute walks after meals controlled sugar **after dinner** better than one 45-minute walk. Over the whole day, both schedules worked about equally.
     - **SAY in your words:** *"These are effects on after-meal sugar in small studies. Not your HbA1c, and not a cure."*
   - **2. Easy is enough** (F3). Start within about half an hour of eating, at a comfortable pace. Short experiments suggest light walking beats sitting, but they don't set a minimum dose for you.
   - **3. Dinner is the one to keep.** It's often the biggest meal, and it's where the study saw the biggest difference.
   - *(Question: "What about the '52%' I see on YouTube?" Answer: that number comes from a lab test of one calf muscle working steadily, not from a few minutes of walking.)*
6. **Careful / emergency:**
   - If you take **insulin or sulfonylurea tablets**, walking can drop your sugar too low. Ask your doctor, and carry something sweet.
   - Stop and get checked if walking brings chest pressure, unusual breathlessness or dizziness.
   - Indoors is fine: a hallway, good shoes, a rail if balance is poor.
   - **SAY AS WRITTEN:** both lines (A.6).
7. **Story ending:** what the after-dinner readings did when the walk moved.
8. **Questions to ask your doctor:**
   - Should I check my sugar 1–2 hours after dinner?
   - Can my diabetes medicine make my sugar too low when I walk?
   - Is my heart fit for daily walking?
   - **Next video:** "How fast do you walk at 70?"

**Never say:** "drops sugar by 52%", "2 minutes is enough", "replaces your medicine", "cures diabetes".

- **Why this works:** competitors argue over a number ("120/80 MYTH", "140/90 MYTH"). Ours shows a check the viewer can do.

**Card**
1. **Scene:** "You get up from the sofa and everything goes dark for a second. You sit down and check your BP, and it's fine. So why does it happen?"
   - **Ask on camera:** *"Is it spinning, or does it go dark and faint-like?"*
   - **SAY in your words:** *"Spinning, especially when you turn in bed, is often an inner-ear problem. Going dark when you stand can be your blood pressure. Today is about the second one."*

   - **1. What it is** (F8): a drop of 20 or more in the top number, **or** 10 in the bottom, within 3 minutes of standing.

   - **2. The check** (CDC STEADI protocol). Show the log on camera:
     - Lie down for 5 minutes, then measure BP **and pulse**.
     - Stand, if it's safe and with someone holding you, then measure at **1 minute** and at **3 minutes**.
     - Write down the three readings and how you felt.

     - Medicines that can contribute: alpha-blockers (doxazosin, terazosin, and the prostate pill tamsulosin), some beta-blockers, some other BP pills, and water pills, especially if you're dehydrated.
     - The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline's goal for most older adults living at home is below 130/80, but it's tailored. The guideline says better BP control can actually **reduce** this standing drop.
     - **SAY in your words:** *"I can't give you a dose or a 'too low' number in a video. Your doctor sets your target from your symptoms and your readings."*

   - Fainting, chest pain, a racing heart, or new weakness or speech trouble.
   - A fall with a head injury while taking a blood thinner.
   - **SAY AS WRITTEN:** both lines (A.6).
7. **Story ending:** what the standing reading showed and what the doctor adjusted.
8. **Questions to ask your doctor:**
   - Can you check my BP lying and standing?
   - Could any of my medicines cause this?
   - What BP target is right for me?
   - **Next video:** "Waking up with chest pressure."

**Never say:** "the ideal BP after 65 is 150", "120/80 is too low for seniors", "stop your pill if you're dizzy".
