The Netherlands, home to the largest pension system in the entire European Union,
just finished rolling out the first wave of a historic overhaul
across nearly 2 trillion euros in assets under management.
The first wave was completed on January 1st this year.
It included 30 Dutch pension funds and 10 million members.
All were moved from the old collective-defined benefit model
to an individual-defined contribution model.
Under the old rules, those Dutch pension funds had to match every promise with an asset.
So they bought ultra-long German bonds, French bonds, and long-dated swaps.
And they bought them regardless of the yield.
They bought them because a regulator told them they had to,
which is how Dutch pension funds came to own an estimated 10% of the entire German sovereign bond market.
But under the new rules, they don't have to be a buyer anymore.
The Dutch Central Bank estimates these funds could unwind 100 to 150 billion euros in long-dated bonds and swaps.
PIMCO measured the impact of this,
and their base case estimate is the equivalent of 115 billion euros of 30-year bond demand
simply vanishing from the long end.
And the biggest wave hasn't even hit yet.
One fund, ABP, the largest,
holds about a third of the entire Dutch pension system on its own,
more than 500 billion euros.
It converts next January.
