America's own central bank said the same thing.
The desk that runs the Federal Reserve's bond portfolio
told the committee that ownership of treasury securities
had shifted from relatively price-insensitive
official sector holders
to more price-sensitive private investors,
and that this could have implications for the term premium.
And the term premium is just the new buyer's
extra charge on top for tying their money up
for 30 years instead of 30 days.
The United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility
also found a similar thing happening
to their government bonds, known as gilts.
Today, British-defined benefit pension funds
hold gilts worth about 27% of the entire British economy.
They estimate that falls to under 6% over the long term,
and shifts this size cause damage.
It's estimated that weaker pension demand
could push the interest rate on British government debt
up by around 80 basis points,
which would cost the British government
about 22 billion pounds per year.
So this is a global trend we're seeing,
that ripples through every major economy.
It's not that investors refuse
to lend governments money anymore.
We're still seeing auctions clear.
It's that the buyers governments once knew and loved
got replaced by buyers who care about the price they pay,
and they want a better price.
