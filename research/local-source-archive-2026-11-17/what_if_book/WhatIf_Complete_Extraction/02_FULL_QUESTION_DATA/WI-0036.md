# WI-0036: Glass Half Empty

**Question:** What if a glass of water was, all of a sudden, literally half empty?

**Submitted by:** Vittorio Iacovella

**PDF pages:** 134–139

**Status:** VISUALLY_REVIEWED; SOURCE_OCR_NEEDS_PROOFREAD

## Answer summary

A vacuum above water collapses harmlessly compared with a vacuum below it, which leads to water hammer and a shattered glass.

## Scientific explanation

The answer compares an ordinary half-water half-air glass, a glass with vacuum replacing air above water, and water suspended over a lower vacuum. Initially molecules barely move on the microsecond scale. Fast air molecules enter the exposed upper vacuum, while water at either vacuum interface starts evaporating. By a few hundred microseconds incoming air fills the top vacuum, strikes the water, and launches a pressure wave; the glass flexes but holds. The sealed lower vacuum cannot admit air. Atmospheric pressure pushes the water and glass together, lifting the glass by about 2 ms. A little water vapor accumulates but provides too little cushioning. Under 10 ms, water and bottom approach at several m/s; water's near-incompressibility makes their collision an intense impulse that breaks the bottom. The upper glass continues upward, strikes the ceiling, and falls as fragments. This is the same water-hammer mechanism used in the bottle-bottom party trick.
