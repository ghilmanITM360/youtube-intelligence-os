# WI-0051: Self-Fertilization

**Question:** I read about some researchers who were trying to produce sperm from bone marrow stem cells. If a woman were to have sperm cells made from her own stem cells and impregnate herself, what would be her relationship to her daughter?

**Submitted by:** R Scott LaMorte

**PDF pages:** 173–182

## Answer summary

The hypothetical child inherits only the parent's variants, but loses much of their paired diversity; the source estimates an inbreeding coefficient of 0.5.

## Scientific explanation

The source reviews preliminary stem-cell-to-sperm research, distinguishing spermatogonial or sperm-like cells from functional sperm and noting a 2009 paper's retraction for plagiarism. It then uses a deliberately simplified D&D model: seven chromosomes, six stats plus sex; each parent has two variants; gametes randomly select one version. Numbers and multipliers illustrate favorable heterozygous combinations and harmful identical pairs. Self-fertilization independently samples twice from the same parent, so about half the child's loci receive identical variants from one parental copy. No Y donor means a female child in the simplified setup. Recessive harmful variants become more likely to pair, exemplified by spinal muscular atrophy; sickle-cell variation illustrates a heterozygote advantage with homozygote harm. The parent possesses all the child's variants but the child lacks some of the parent's. The resulting inbreeding coefficient is 0.50, likened to several generations of sibling mating, and severe health risks are expected. The analogy is not a literal chromosome-to-stat map; real genetics and reproductive biology are more complex.
