## 2. Script lock and science check
### 2.1 Source draft (verbatim, the owner's text; kept unchanged)
**B01 Hook and early verdict** (19 words): Could you boil a cup of water just by stirring it? Short answer: no. But the spoon does try.

**B02 Stirring adds energy** (29 words): Stirring really does add energy. The swirl rubs the water against itself, and that friction turns motion into heat. So the idea is sound. The problem is the amount.

**B03 Energy and power ladder** (58 words): Take a quarter liter of water at twenty degrees. Boiling is a hundred. Lifting it those eighty degrees takes about eighty-four thousand joules. Do that in two minutes, and you need seven hundred watts going in. That's seven old hundred-watt light bulbs, or about what a microwave draws to heat a mug. In horse terms, about one horsepower.

**B04 Niagara comparison** (16 words): Even dropping the water over Niagara Falls warms it only about a tenth of a degree.

**B05 The spoon adds under one watt** (45 words): Now the spoon. A hard stir adds well under one watt: less than a hundredth of one of those bulbs. Seven hundred needed, under one delivered. The water warms a hair, until the cup leaks that heat away as fast as the spoon adds it.

**B06 Faster stirring: cavitation and splash** (50 words): So stir faster, far faster than any hand: tens of thousands of strokes a second, and the water cavitates. Pressure drops behind the spoon and vapor bubbles form. That looks like boiling, but it's low pressure, not heat. And the splashing spreads the water out, so it cools even faster.

**B07 Measured tea cooling** (21 words): Someone actually measured tea: stirring it, dipping the spoon, or leaving it alone all cool it at about the same rate.

**B08 Answer and sugar** (29 words): So, ordinary spoon, open cup? No. The needle stays near room temperature, however hard you stir. A spoon is for dissolving sugar. Boiling is not in its job description.
