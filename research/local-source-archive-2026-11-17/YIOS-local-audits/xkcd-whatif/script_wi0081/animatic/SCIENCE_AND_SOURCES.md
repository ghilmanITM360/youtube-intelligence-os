## Numerical checks
- 0.25 kg water × 4.18 kJ/(kg K) × 80 K = 83.6 kJ; 83.6 kJ / 120 s = 697 W. This is ideal heat delivered to the water, ignores cup heating and environmental losses, assumes water boils at 100°C at approximately one atmosphere, and does not include latent heat to vaporize appreciable water. Seven 100 W light bulbs is an electrical power analogy, not seven bulbs heating the mug with perfect efficiency. One mechanical horsepower is about 746 W. Munroe's starting calculation: https://what-if.xkcd.com/71/ .
- The microwave comparison needs qualification: manufacturers often rate microwave *output* at 700 W, while input drawn from the wall is greater. Proposed correction: 'about the heating output of a small microwave'.
- Niagara: gh/c ≈ 9.81 × 51 / 4180 ≈ 0.12°C, assuming all potential energy thermalizes in the water and ignoring other exchanges. Actual measured heating need not equal that ideal upper estimate. Proposed correction: 'could warm by roughly a tenth of a degree in an idealized calculation.' https://what-if.xkcd.com/71/ .
- Stirring power: the article estimates 10^-7 W by an industrial-mixer extrapolation, not a direct measurement of the depicted hand and spoon. The script's firm 'well under one watt' is not established for every hard stir. Proposed correction: 'A typical hand stir supplies far less power than 700 watts; the exact amount depends on how you stir.' Comparative graphic is conditional on the narrated under-1-W assumption, not a separately verified measurement.
- Cavitation depends on local pressure relative to vapor pressure and on nuclei, spoon geometry, speed and water conditions. The source's tens-of-thousands-of-stirs-per-second is an estimate, not a general threshold. Proposed correction: 'At sufficiently high local speeds, the pressure behind the spoon can fall enough for cavitation.' https://ittc.info/media/1465/water-quality.pdf .
- Splashing may increase evaporation and convective heat transfer; direction and magnitude depend on conditions. For water initially near ambient, 'cools even faster' is not a universal experimental prediction. Proposed correction: 'Splashing exposes more water to air and can increase evaporative cooling.'
- Munroe cites an informal measured tea experiment with similar curves and somewhat faster spoon dipping; not a universal biological or physical law. https://what-if.xkcd.com/71/ (cites underlying experiment). A separate student theoretical calculation also predicts a small effect under its assumptions, not a replication: https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/pst/article/download/2223/2127/3238 . Proposed correction: 'In one home experiment, the cooling curves were similar, with dipping slightly faster.'
- Cavitation uses localized vapor bubbles behind the moving spoon; boiling diagram shows bubbles rising from the pot bottom above a burner. The depiction is schematic. Sugar dissolves rather than disappears chemically.
