Across one month, Japan mounted the largest currency defense in its history, $11.7 trillion yen, or nearly $74 billion spent in four weeks holding up its own money, and that money came from selling down its American bond holdings, and it worked, for about three weeks. Then reality set in. Japan's benchmark rate now sits around 1%, while America sits between 3.5 and 3.3 quarters. A bonds price and the interest rate on it move in opposite directions. Flood the market with American bonds nobody asked for, the price drops, and the government that issued them has to pay more to borrow. So Japan sells American debt. American borrowing costs go up. The gap between the two countries gets wider, and capital still goes wherever it gets paid the most, so it goes to America. Every dollar Tokyo raised to push the yen up made the reason the yen was falling in the first place slightly worse. This is what's known as the FX intervention feedback loop, or the doom loop. It's a machine that eats its own tail. Every force pushing the yen down before the operation was still pushing it down after.
