Forget interest rates for a second. There's a second lever. This one's hidden in the back of Jerome Powell's closet, and it only comes out in case of an emergency. It's a literal money printer. When the economy's dying, like in 2020, the Fed creates money out of thin air and uses it to buy bonds, pushing that new money into the system to keep it alive. That flood of new money is called quantitative easing. And in the pandemic, they ran it harder than at any point in history. The Fed's balance sheet went from around $4 trillion to nearly $9 trillion in about two years, leading to inflation spiking to over 9% in June 2022. But when inflation caught fire, the Fed was forced to do something they've only done twice in its more than 100-year history. They ran the money printer in reverse. Instead of creating money to buy bonds, the Fed let the bonds it already held expire and didn't replace them every month, a little less money in the system than the month before. This policy's called quantitative tightening, and it started in June of 2022. Over the next three and a half years, the Fed took more than $2.2 trillion off its balance sheet. But then Jerome Powell snuck back out of the closet and turned the engine back on. 11 days later, on December 12th, the Fed started buying bonds again, around $40 billion a month. The balance sheet it spent three and a half years draining is now refilling.
