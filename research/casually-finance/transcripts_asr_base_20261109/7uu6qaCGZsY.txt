If there's a hole, someone's going to fill it. But before I jump into how private credit plugs a hole in the market, let's back up for a second. In the early 2000s, the private credit market was small. About 140 billion globally. It was the weird sibling of private equity, still niche, and still ignored. Then came 2008. Over the years, banks have been handing out loans like candy on Halloween. Started with the infamous Ninja loans, which were loans to people with no income, no job, and no assets. Then banks started bundling these steaming piles of debt into collateralized debt obligations or CDOs. And we're selling these as securities products. And if that wasn't reckless enough, they invented CDO squared, which was essentially just ticking time bombs disguised as investment products. And shocker, it all came crashing down. The banks had blown up the economy by handing out loans to anyone with a pulse. So following the financial crisis, regulators stepped in and said, it's time we start acting like responsible adults. These regulators rolled out a whole new rulebook that forced banks to increase capital reserves, cut leverage, and tighten lending standards. This meant that lending to small and medium-sized companies, which were the companies with higher risk profiles, suddenly wasn't worth the headache for traditional banks anymore. And so by 2010, traditional bank lending to these middle market companies had plummeted. But here's the problem, these middle market companies still need money. And now they were in an awkward spot. These middle market companies were too big for your local community banks and too small to issue bonds on Wall Street. And that's where private credit steps in. Private credit funds stepped in and said, don't worry, the banks may have ghosted you, but we've got you. Forget the collateral. Just sign here, pay 10% interest, and we'll tack on a few fees. And companies lined up faster than white girls at a Lulu lemon sale. In 2010, private credit assets under management sat around $300 billion. And by 2020, this number had surpassed $1 trillion. That means since 2007, private credit hasn't just grown, it's exploded more than 10 fold. It's turned from what was once a niche corner of finance into Wall Street's favorite money machine. Over the last 20 years, private credit has returned about 9.6% annually. While over the same period, the stock market has returned about 10.2% annually. So if private credit has underperformed when compared to the stock market, why are investors drooling over this asset class? And the answer comes down to two words. Two words that make business majors whisper, this is it boys. Two words that make that girl a couple desks over from you say, maybe he's not such a nerd. Two words that make investors salivate just at the thought. And the two words are stable returns. See, while private credit may trail the stock market by about 60 basis points annually, it delivers those returns without the emotional roller coaster that stocks bring. Stocks can swing 20% up or down in a single year, or a single week if Jerome Powell so much as clears his throat, while private credit on the other hand is smooth, boring, and consistent. And after over a decade of interest rates being garbage, where bonds paid next to nothing, private credit has become the coldilocks asset. Two risky for banks, two safe for hedge funds, and just right for anyone managing 30 trillion in other people's money. It offers pension funds and endowments a place to park their 30 trillion in assets under management. It's a seemingly perfect middle ground solution with equity like returns and bond like volatility. However, it's not all sunshine and rainbows. Because while on the surface, private credit may sound like the perfect investment vehicle, it carries its own set of risks, specifically around liquidity, market market valuations, defaults, and leverage. When it comes to private credit, there's no centralized exchange, no liquidity, no public price quotes, and nobody really knows how much these loans are worth until someone stops paying. And even Wall Street investors will happily ignore any growing cracks underneath the surface, as long as they're getting their 8-10% a year. Because the whole private credit industry is run on one assumption. Borers will keep paying, and to be fair for the last decade, well they have.
