The largest project financing ever completed as an investment grade bond, $27 billion. All riding on a special purpose vehicle named after a New Orleans fried pastry. The company is called Benye Investor. It exists for exactly one purpose. To own a data center campus called Hyperion, a multi-gigawatt site in Richland, Parish, rural Louisiana. And Hyperion is a meta project. Meta announced it, meta designs it, meta builds it, meta operates it, meta is the only tenant it will ever have. But the $27 billion of debt that built the Hyperion data center campus belongs to the pastry. Here's how it works. Blue Owl Capital, a private investment firm, creates the special purpose vehicle called Benye Investor and puts up 80% of the equity. Meta takes the other 20% and signs on as the building's only tenant, then the pastry goes borrowing. Benye issues $27.3 billion of investment grade bonds. The bondholders who are some of the biggest money managers on earth like Black Rock and Pimco, wire in the cash, Benye takes that cash and builds Hyperion. Then the final step, meta moves in and pays rent. And that rent is what pays the bondholders back year after year all the way to 2049. So it's Meta's data center, Meta's machines, Meta's engineers inside it. But Meta legally owns just 20% of it while Blue Owl owns the other 80. Why? It's because of a single phrase in the accounting rules. At 20% meta is not the primary beneficiary of the venture. And if you're not the primary beneficiary, the venture's $27 billion of debt is not your debt. So it vanishes from your balance sheet legally.
