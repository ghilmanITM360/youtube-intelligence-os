Data centers don't want power eventually. They want it now, guaranteed forever. PJM is the largest grid operator in America, serving 67 million people across 13 states from Chicago to Washington, DC, roughly one in five Americans. If you live anywhere in that footprint, your lights are on because of decisions they make, and PJM's biggest decision of the year happens at an event nobody watches. Once a year, PJM holds an auction to pay power plants to promise they'll be ready with electricity on the worst days of the year. It's essentially a billion dollar pinky promise that there's going to be electricity when there's a January freeze or an August heat wave. For most of this century, that insurance was dirt cheap because American electricity demand flatlined for 20 years. It made the capacity auction the single most boring event in American finance. Then, the data centers arrived. Not one at a time, by the dozen. Buildings that each swallow as much electricity as a small city, walking into a system designed for a world where demand barely grew. For the first time in history, PJM went shopping with more money than ever before and paid the maximum price allowed under federal law, but there still wasn't enough power supply to go around. And that's the physical reality of the grid. All that money can't summon a gas turbine, a transformer, or a transmission line out of thin air when the world's manufacturers are booked years out in advance, which means the grid failed to buy what it needed and then paid full price for what it got.
