So there's two things Japan can do with its war chest of American bonds. It can sell them, or it can pawn them. Japan's choice was to start selling. Japan spent a record amount of money sold American debt to do it and told the entire world it meant business, and not only did Japan's way not work. It also was now America's problem. There's a tool at the Federal Reserve where a foreign government can hand over its American bonds, take dollars in exchange, and buy the bonds back later at a slightly higher repurchase price. It's a pawn shop for governments. The official name for it is the Foreign and International Monetary Authority's repo facility. These are liquidity tools for central banks that run on short-term repurchase agreements. Here's how it works. A foreign central bank, like the Bank of Japan, hands over $100 in U.S. bonds to the Federal Reserve. In return, the Fed gives them $100 in cash. Then the next day, the Bank of Japan buys those bonds back for $101. That $1 difference between the sale price and the repurchase price is the interest earned by the Federal Reserve. The whole reason for the facility is to give foreign countries quick access to dollars, so they aren't forced to dump American debt onto the open market. It gives Japan a way to raise dollars without ever becoming a seller. It's what you build for a creditor you cannot afford to lose.
