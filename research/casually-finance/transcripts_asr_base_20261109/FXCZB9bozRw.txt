Let's pretend you own Munder Difflin, the biggest paper mill in town. You've got a star employee, his name's Dwight, 20 years on the job, never missed a shift, and a beat farmer on the weekend. Solid guy. The deal at your mill is the standard for this time period, work here long enough, and when you retire, the company pays you $2,000 a month. Every month, for the rest of your life, it's called a pension. For Dwight, it's a great deal. He never has to think about running out of money again. For you, the owner of the paper mill, it's a new bill, and not a bill you pay once. A bill that starts the day Dwight retires and doesn't stop until the day he dies. Might be 15 years, might be 35, nobody knows. That's the part that makes this hard. If you knew Dwight needed exactly 20 years of checks, you could set aside exactly 20 years of money and be done with it. But you don't know how long the beats are going to keep him going. So your pension problem has a very specific shape. You owe a fixed amount, on a fixed schedule, stretching decades out, which means whatever you buy to cover it needs that same shape. That rules out almost everything. You can't put Dwight's retirement in stocks. Stocks build wealth over time. Dwight needs $2,000 in cash on the first of the month, guaranteed, and you can't take much risk with it because it isn't your money. You already promised it. If the market has a bad year, Dwight's check size doesn't get any smaller. So you end up buying the most boring instrument in existence. Something that pays a set amount, on a set date, and has no option to stop paying. A 30-year government bond. You line its payments up against Dwight's payments, and the two schedules cancel each other out. You couldn't care less about what the bond you bought is worth tomorrow. You didn't buy that bond as an investment. You bought it to cover Dwight's pension. That's the Defined Benefit Pension Fund. They were patient, had deep pockets, were legally required to show up, and completely unbothered by price. For 50 years, traditional pension funds were the best customers of the long end of the bond market. But they're the last of a dying breed.
