What we're watching is a classic kindergarten game of, he said, she said, when a company buys a machine, it spreads the cost across the years the machine stays useful, called the useful life. That schedule is called depreciation. Stretch the useful life out, and your annual depreciation drops, making profits look bigger today, shrink the years, and your annual depreciation expense rises, lowering your reported profits, which means one boring accounting guess, how long does a chip stay useful? Quietly determines billions in big tech earnings, and the problems arise when nobody agrees on the answer. In January 2024, Amazon decided its servers would last six years, instead of five. That single decision reduced its depreciation expense by $3.2 billion, and added $2.5 billion to its profit for the year. Twelve months later, Amazon changed its mind. They walked the estimate from six years back down to five for subsets of their servers and networking equipment. The reason Amazon gave in the filing was the increased pace of technology development, particularly in artificial intelligence. And while Amazon was walking some things back, Meta went the opposite direction. Meta extended the life of its servers and network assets to five and a half years. That single change reduced its 2025 depreciation costs by $2.9 billion, and added roughly a dollar to Meta's earnings per share. So same technology, same time period, two accounting departments looking at the same silicon and reaching opposite conclusions.
