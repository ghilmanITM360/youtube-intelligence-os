## Script

This question comes from Rob, who asks: is it possible to build a jetpack using downward firing rifles?

I was sort of surprised to find that the answer was yes!

The principle here is pretty simple. If you fire a bullet forward, the recoil pushes you back.

So if you fire downward, the recoil should push you up.

The first question we have to answer is “can a rapidly firing gun even lift its own weight?”

If a gun weighs ten pounds but only produces eight pounds of recoil when continuously firing, it won’t be able to lift itself off the ground, let alone lift itself plus a person.

In the aerospace world, the ratio between a craft’s thrust and its weight is called, appropriately, its thrust-to-weight ratio.

If it’s less than 1 –that is, if the thrust is less than the weight– the vehicle can’t take off.

The Saturn V had a thrust-to-weight ratio at takeoff of about 1.5.

There are a lot of repeating firearms, but it turns out a lot of them also tend to come in at a thrust-to-weight ratio of around 1.5.

This means if you stood one on end and remotely fired it, it would rise into the air while firing.

The amount of thrust created by a rocket (or a rapidly firing gun) depends on one: how much mass it’s throwing out behind it, and two: how fast it’s throwing that mass.

Thrust is the product of these two numbers.

A self-reloading firearm might weigh around 10 pounds. If you fire ten 10g bullets per second going 700 meters per second, the thrust from the stream of bullets is around 15 pounds.

But this is just the bullets – the actual thrust can be up to around 30% higher.

The reason is that a rifle isn’t just spitting out bullets – it’s also spitting out hot gas and explosive debris, and the precise amount of extra force will vary depending on the specific design of the rifle and the cartridge.

The overall efficiency of using a rifle for propulsion also depends on whether you eject the shell casings out of the vehicle or carry them along with you.

Let’s assume we eject them for maximum efficiency – if we’re firing bullets at the ground we clearly have a complete lack of regard for the safety of anyone below us anyway.

So what does all this mean for our jetpack?

Well, a self-reloading rifle can take off, but only generates about five extra pounds of thrust beyond its own weight, so it can’t lift much more than a squirrel, let alone a human rider.

Let’s try multiple rifles. If one rifle can lift itself plus five extra pounds, then two rifles can lift ten extra pounds.

At this point, it’s clear where we’re headed: with 30 rifles, we can just barely lift the weight of a 150lb person – they’ll be able to hover, but not really rise into the air. 40 rifles will accelerate that person upwards at 0.1 gees; 60 rifles will get them to 0.2 gees.

If we add enough rifles, the weight of the passenger gets spread out over so many of them that it becomes irrelevant and the craft’s thrust-to-weight ratio approaches that of a single, unburdened rifle – an upwards acceleration of 5m/s^2, or half a gee.

But that acceleration won’t last very long, because we have another problem: Fuel.

I mean, ammunition.

There’s no advantage to carrying more than about 150 rounds of ammunition.

And the reason for this is a fundamental and central problem in rocket science: Fuel makes you heavier.

Each bullet weighs 10 grams, and the cartridge might weigh around twice that.

If we add more than about 150 rounds, the rifle will be too heavy to take off.

This suggests our optimal craft comprises a large number of rifles (ideally at least 300) carrying 150 rounds of ammunition each, for 15 seconds of continuous firing.

This craft could accelerate upward to vertical speeds of around 80 miles an hour, and after it ran out of ammunition –I mean fuel– it would continue coasting upwards to a height of about 200 meters.

So we’ve answered Rob’s question.

With enough rifles, you could fly.

But our rig is clearly not a practical jetpack. Can we do better?

The GAU-8 Avenger is an autocannon (which is a giant self-reloading gun usually attached to a vehicle) that fires a one-pound bullet up to sixty times a second.

It produces almost five tons of recoil force, which is extraordinary considering that it’s mounted in a type of plane whose engines produce only a combined eight tons of thrust.

If you put two of these cannons in one aircraft and fired both of them forward while trying to take off, the guns would overpower the engines and you’d accelerate backward.

To put it another way: If I mounted a GAU-8 Avenger on my car, put the car in neutral, and started firing backward from a standstill, I would be breaking the interstate speed limit in less than three seconds.

As good as this autocannon would be as a jetpack engine, the Soviets built one that was even more powerful.

The GSh-6-30 weighs half as much as the GAU-8 and has a higher rate of fire.

Its thrust-to-weight ratio approaches 30, which means if you pointed one at the ground and fired, not only would it take off in a rapidly expanding spray of deadly metal fragments, but you would experience 30 gees of acceleration.

This is way too much.

In fact, even when it was firmly mounted in an aircraft, the acceleration was a problem.

To quote: “The recoil… had a tendency to inflict damage on the aircraft.

Landing lights almost always broke after firing.

Firing more than about 30 rounds in a burst was asking for trouble from overheating” But if you somehow braced the human rider, made the craft strong enough to survive the acceleration, wrapped it in an aerodynamic shell, and made sure it was adequately cooled… with a GSH-6-30, you could jump over mountains.
