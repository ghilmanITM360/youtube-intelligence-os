Script

HOOK (0:00-0:15) "The U.S. government says it will put one thousand dollars into an account for certain children. I ran the math on what that thousand dollars could become by age eighteen, with fees, using only the official rules. And the answer depends on one number that nobody can promise you."

PROMISE (0:15-0:40) "If you have a child born between 2025 and 2028, or you're thinking about it, this is for you. By the end you'll know who qualifies, the one form you have to file, and what a thousand dollars can grow to under three honest scenarios. I'll show every formula on screen."

SOURCE ON SCREEN (0:40-1:20) "Everything I use here comes from the IRS and Treasury pages, which I'll put in the description. [VERIFY: show the irs.gov Trump Accounts FAQ page on screen, with today's date]. Where official sources and news articles disagree, I'll say so."

SECTION 1 (1:20-3:30): Who gets the $1,000. "Rule one: the child must be a U.S. citizen with a valid Social Security number, and born between January 1st, 2025 and December 31st, 2028 [VERIFY: IRS FAQ]. Rule two: it isn't automatic. [VERIFY: IRS FAQ on auto-enrollment vs election, the sources differ] You claim the thousand dollars by filing Form 4547, the Trump Account Election, and choosing the pilot program option [VERIFY]. You can do that through the Trump Accounts app, the website, or your IRS online account [VERIFY]. If you do nothing, you may not get the pilot money. [VERIFY: wording on screen]"

SECTION 2 (3:30-5:30): Where the money goes. "The money has to be invested in a broad U.S. stock index fund or ETF, with no leverage, and the law caps the fund's expense ratio at 0.10 percent a year [VERIFY: statute / Treasury]. That is 10 cents per hundred dollars each year. Now, here is the catch worth knowing: that cap applies to the fund. Some critics argue other administrative fees might not be capped [VERIFY: this is an opinion; check the primary text before saying it]. I can't confirm either way from the official pages I read, so I'm flagging it."

SECTION 3 (5:30-8:30): The math. "Here is the formula: one thousand times one plus the return, minus the fee, raised to the eighteenth power. I'm using three made-up returns: four, six, and eight percent, because nobody knows the real one. With the 0.10 percent fee: at four percent, about nineteen hundred ninety-one dollars. At six percent, about twenty-eight hundred. At eight percent, about thirty-nine hundred thirty. Now watch what a one percent fee does to the same money: at six percent you drop from twenty-eight hundred to about twenty-four hundred. That's the cost of fees over eighteen years, and it's why the fee cap matters. To be clear: these are not predictions. Stock returns can be negative for years, and I'm ignoring taxes and inflation."

SECTION 4 (8:30-10:00): What you can and can't do with it. "Before the child turns eighteen, withdrawals are generally not allowed [VERIFY]. After eighteen, most of the special rules end and it works more like an IRA, and withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income [VERIFY]. One more thing the sources disagree on: whether the growth period ends on January 1st of the year the child turns eighteen, or at eighteen itself [VERIFY: Treasury]. I won't guess."

ONE ACTION (10:00-10:40) "If your child may qualify, check one thing this week: whether the pilot election has been made. Look it up on the official IRS page, not on a social media post."

CLOSE (10:40-11:00) "All sources are in the description. This is information, not financial advice. Next video: I tried to cancel eight subscriptions and counted every click."
