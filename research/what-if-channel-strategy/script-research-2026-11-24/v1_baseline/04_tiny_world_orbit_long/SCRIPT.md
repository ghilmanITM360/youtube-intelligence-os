## Voice-over

Orbit sounds like a place. A high, quiet place, far above everything. It is not. Orbit is a speed. Move sideways fast enough, and the ground curves away from you as quickly as you fall toward it.

On Earth, that speed is about twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour. Nobody jumps that. But the speed you need shrinks with the size of the world. So how small would a world have to be for one jump to do it?

Let's build one. A ball of ordinary rock, about two thousand kilograms per cubic metre. No air. No spin. No hills. A perfectly smooth, perfectly round, tiny planet. That is a model, not a real asteroid. Real ones are lumpy and spinning. But it lets us do the arithmetic honestly.

Make it three kilometres in radius. Gravity at the surface would be less than two ten-thousandths of Earth's. A whole person would press on the ground about as hard as a couple of coins do on Earth.

On that world, the speed for a grazing orbit is about two metres per second. A little over two. That is about eight kilometres an hour, a gentle jog.

Now the ladder. Launch level with the ground at ninety-nine percent of that speed, and you come down a little further on. At exactly that speed, you skim all the way around. Ten percent faster, and you swing out to about one and a half kilometres above the ground, and still come back. At about forty percent faster, a little over three metres per second, you leave for good. Three very different outcomes, and the first two are one percent apart.

So how do you get to two metres per second? Not by running. You have almost no weight, so your feet barely grip. Even with perfect grip, it would take about twenty minutes of sprinting. That is a very long warm-up for a jump.

So jump. A strong jump on Earth leaves the ground at around two to three metres per second. If we assume the same take-off speed on a small world, which is an assumption, a world three to four kilometres in radius puts orbit within reach.

But here is the catch. A jump goes up as well as forward, and an orbit that starts at the ground has its lowest point right where you left. Tilt the launch up by half a degree, and the path dips about twenty-six metres underground. Five degrees, and it dips more than two hundred and fifty metres. The path runs into the ground, so you come back down, about half a lap later.

An ordinary jump near that speed, tilted upward, is a long, slow hop of roughly an hour. Only a launch that is almost exactly level can work.

Suppose you manage one. How long is the lap? On a smooth ball, the time for a grazing lap depends only on how dense the world is, not how big. At this density, it is about two hours and twenty minutes. On an airless world with Earth's density, it would be about eighty-four.

And you would not float free. You would arrive back where you started, skimming the ground at the same level. Your feet began about a metre up, so any bump taller than that stops you.

So, could one sideways jump put you in orbit? On this idealised world, yes, but only barely. It has to be level, fast enough, and the ground has to stay out of the way. An ordinary jump takes you on a hop, and you land.

Real small worlds are lumpy, they spin, and they have neighbours pulling on them. All of that changes the answer. But the lesson holds. Orbit is sideways speed, not height, and on a small enough world, that speed fits in a person.
