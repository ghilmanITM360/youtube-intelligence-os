## Voice-over

Imagine the Moon is simply gone. No explosion, no debris. One night it is there, the next it is not. Earth keeps spinning once a day and keeps circling the Sun. What happens to the oceans?

First, nothing dramatic. The sea does not drain, and it does not flood. The Moon was never holding the water up. But the tides are another matter, and to see why, we need to look at how tides really work.

The Moon does not simply drag the water toward itself. What matters is how its pull varies across the planet. It pulls harder on the side facing it than on the far side, and that difference stretches the ocean into two bulges, one toward the Moon and one away. Earth spins through them, so many coasts get two high tides a day.

Now remove the Moon. The stretching from the Moon goes with it. But the water does not know that yet. Tidal motion is moving water, and moving water keeps moving. The old rhythm would fade, not stop. How long that takes depends on how the oceans shed that motion, and this video cannot give you a number.

So what is left? The Sun, which stretches the oceans too. Its pull on Earth is about a hundred and eighty times the Moon's, yet it is the weaker tide-maker. The reason is distance. The Sun is almost four hundred times farther away, so it pulls on the near and far sides of Earth almost equally, and only that small difference stretches the water. By that measure, the Sun is about forty-six percent as strong as the Moon.

So tides do not vanish. They shrink. In an ideal ocean spread evenly over Earth, the solar tide would be a bit under half the lunar one. But real oceans have basins, coastlines and their own natural rhythms, and those change each place differently. So there is no single number. What we can say is that today's tides contain both pushes, and without the Moon, only one remains.

Timing changes too. Today, high tide arrives about fifty minutes later each day, because the Moon moves across the sky. Without it, the main rhythm would follow the Sun, which repeats every twelve hours exactly. On a given beach, high tides would come at about the same times each day, instead of drifting around the clock. Not the same on every coast, though. Each coast has its own offset, and some places have more complicated tides. The beach would still have a clock. It would just be the Sun's.

Something else would go. Today, tides swell when the Sun and Moon line up and shrink when they sit at right angles. That fortnightly swing needs two tide-makers. With one, it disappears.

There are things this video does not answer. What would happen to Earth's tilt, to the seasons, or to the length of the day? Those are separate questions, and some of them are still debated. Guessing would be easy. It would not be honest.

So here is the answer. The oceans would not drain, and the tides would not vanish. They would lose the Moon's rhythm and most of their size, the beach clock would lock to the Sun, and the monthly swing would disappear. Exactly how much each coast changes is a job for ocean models and tide gauges, not for one video.
