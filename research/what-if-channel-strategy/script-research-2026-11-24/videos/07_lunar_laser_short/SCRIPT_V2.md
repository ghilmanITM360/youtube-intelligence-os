## Voice-over

A laser fired at the Moon returns about two and a half seconds later. That is lunar laser ranging, and it works because mirror arrays were left on the Moon.

Three Apollo crews placed arrays of small corner mirrors, and two Soviet rovers carried others. Each mirror sends light straight back toward where it came from.

Light needs about a second and a quarter each way, but the Moon's distance changes, so the round trip runs from about two point four to two point seven seconds.

At the Apache Point station, one pulse carries about three hundred quadrillion photons, and very few come back. At older stations, the detected return was far below one photon per pulse. In record Apache Point runs, it was about one to three. So stations add up many returns, and with hundreds or thousands of photons, they pin down the distance to a few millimetres, approaching one in the best cases. That millimetre describes the precision of the averaged measurement, not a guarantee of accuracy.
