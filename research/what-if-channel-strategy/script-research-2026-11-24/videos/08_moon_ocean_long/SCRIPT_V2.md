## Voice-over

Imagine the Moon is simply gone. No explosion, no debris. One night it is there, the next it is not. Earth keeps spinning and keeps circling the Sun. What happens to the oceans?

Start with what the Moon was not doing. It was not holding the sea in place. Earth's own gravity does that, and Earth's gravity does not change. What the Moon does is stretch the oceans a little, and that stretching is what we call tides.

Here is how. The Moon pulls harder on the side of Earth facing it than on the far side. That difference in pull, not the pull itself, stretches the water toward two bulges, one toward the Moon and one away. Earth rotates under them. On an idealised planet covered by one deep ocean, every place would get two high tides a day.

Real Earth has continents. NOAA explains that they block the westward movement of the bulges as Earth rotates, so tides form complicated patterns inside each ocean basin. Some coasts get two highs a day, some get one, some get a mixture.

Now remove the Moon. The push the Moon supplied stops at once. But water that is already moving does not know that. Tidal motion carries energy, and energy takes time to drain away. How long the old pattern would take to fade, we did not find in any source, so we will not guess.

What remains is the Sun. It pulls on Earth more than one hundred and seventy times harder than the Moon does, yet it makes the smaller tides. Tides come from differences in pull across the planet, and the Sun is nearly four hundred times farther away, so its pull barely changes from one side of Earth to the other. NASA puts the Sun's tide-raising force at a little less than half the Moon's.

That is a statement about the push, not about the water. A push a little less than half as strong does not mean every coast would keep that fraction of its tide. How big the tide is at a given place depends on how the local basin responds. The Bay of Fundy shows this. Its water sloshes with a natural period of about twelve hours, close to the tidal rhythm, and that is part of why its tides are among the largest in the world. A bay with another shape and depth would respond differently to the same push.

We looked for an ocean model of an Earth with no Moon and did not find one. Popular articles give figures, but they disagree and do not show their work. So we will not give you a tide height for any coast.

The rhythm of the push would change, though. Today the Moon's pull follows the lunar day, which is about fifty minutes longer than ours, and that is why high tide usually arrives about fifty minutes later each day. The Sun's push follows the solar day. So the daily slide of tide times that comes from the Moon would no longer be driven by anything. How the Sun's rhythm turns into high and low water on a particular beach is again a local question.

One more thing would go. Today, tides grow when the Sun and Moon line up, at new and full Moon, and shrink when they sit at right angles. That fortnightly swing needs two tide-makers. With one, there is nothing to add up or cancel.

There are questions this video does not answer. What would happen to Earth's tilt, to the seasons, or to the length of the day? Those are separate problems, some still debated, and a confident answer would be a guess.

So, what happens to the ocean if the Moon vanishes? The sea stays on Earth, held by Earth's own gravity. The Moon's stretching stops at once, the tidal motion already in the water fades over a time we cannot state, the Sun's smaller push continues, and how tall the tides would be on any particular coast is a question for ocean models and tide gauges.
