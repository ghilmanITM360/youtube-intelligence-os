## Voice-over

Orbit sounds like a place. A high, quiet place, far above everything. It is a path. Move sideways fast enough, and the ground curves away from you as quickly as you fall toward it, so you keep falling around the world without ever landing.

On Earth that speed is about twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour. Nobody jumps that. But the speed you need shrinks with the size of the world. So how small would a world have to be for a jump to do it?

Let's build a toy world. A ball of ordinary rock, about two thousand kilograms per cubic metre. No air, no spin, no hills, perfectly smooth and round. It is a model, not a real asteroid. Real ones are lumpy and spinning. But it lets us do the arithmetic honestly.

Make it three kilometres in radius. Gravity at the surface is less than two ten-thousandths of Earth's. A person would press on the ground about as hard as a few coins press on a table here.

On that world, the speed for a circular orbit that just skims the surface is about two point two metres per second. That is about eight kilometres an hour, a gentle jog.

Now the ladder. Launch exactly level with the ground at ninety-nine percent of that speed, and you come down a little farther on. At exactly that speed, you skim all the way around. Ten percent faster, and you swing out roughly one point six kilometres above the ground, and still come back. About forty percent faster, a little over three metres per second, and you leave for good.

So how do you reach two point two metres per second? Not by running. With almost no weight, your feet barely grip. Even with perfect grip, it would take about twenty minutes of sprinting.

So jump. A strong jump on Earth leaves the ground at around two to three metres per second. If we assume the same take-off speed on the small world, which is an assumption, a world three to four kilometres in radius puts orbit within reach.

Here is the catch. A jump goes up as well as forward, and an orbit that starts at the ground has its lowest point exactly where you left. Tilt the launch upward by half a degree, and the full path would dip about twenty-six metres below the ground. At five degrees, more than two hundred and fifty metres. The path runs into the world, so you come back down.

So an ordinary jump, tilted upward, becomes a long, slow hop of about an hour. Only a launch that is almost exactly level can work.

Suppose you managed one. How long is the lap? For a circular orbit that just skims a smooth ball, the lap time depends only on how dense the world is, not on how big it is. At two thousand kilograms per cubic metre, it is about two hours and twenty minutes. On an airless world with Earth's average density, it would be about eighty-four minutes.

You would also arrive back where you started, skimming the surface. Your body sits about a metre above your feet, and any bump or boulder in the way would stop you.

So, could one sideways jump put you in orbit? On this idealised world, yes, but only barely: the launch has to be nearly level, fast enough, and the ground has to stay out of the way. An ordinary jump gives you a hop, and you land.

Real small worlds are lumpy, they spin, and neighbouring bodies pull on them. All of that changes the answer, and we have not modelled it. But the principle holds: orbit is sideways speed, not height, and on a small enough world, that speed is within a person's reach, on paper.
