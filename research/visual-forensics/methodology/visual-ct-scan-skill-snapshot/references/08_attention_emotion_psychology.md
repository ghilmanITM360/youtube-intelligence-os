# 08 — Visual salience, attention hypotheses, retention engineering, emotional function

## Evidence-backed wording

Direct observation: "At 00:04.2, red label appears center while other elements dim." Measured change: "Approx. 8% more frame area by 00:04.4 (frame-bounded)." Hypothesis: "That may draw attention from background toward the price." Alternative: "It also may briefly overload the frame." Not permitted: "Viewers certainly look here and retention increases 8%."

## Visual salience stack (hypothesis, not eye-tracking)

Compare motion onset/speed, size, luminance/color contrast, faces/gaze direction, readable labels, center/isolation, empty space, arrows, leading lines, depth changes, prior viewer expectation. Report candidate first/second/third look as proposed salience order with confidence and competing attractors. Genuine eye tracking requires instrumented data.

## Retention mechanisms

Observed structure may support: question/hook, curiosity gap, withheld answer, contrast, timed reveal, expectation violation, novelty, authority cue, illustrated proof, micro-payoff, callback, cliffhanger, pattern interrupt, reorientation, CTA. Repeated rapid cuts are not automatically good and static holds can protect comprehension.

## Emotional engineering

Describe visual stimuli and plausible feelings with alternatives: isolated figure + negative space could communicate loneliness or calm, depending context. Avoid simplistic color→emotion laws or real dopamine loop claims. Consumer response requires human testing.

## Attention timeline output

`Time | visible stimulus | state change | predicted salience | payoff/question | potential fatigue/overload | alternative hypothesis | evidence link | confidence`. Never label an invented *retention percentage* as modeled reality. If a user asks for percentage prediction, explain it is speculative and present risk zones instead.

## Improvement system

For each risk propose a specific editable intervention (shorten hold, improve label contrast, add clear diagram, swap confusing motion for static), cost/benefit tradeoff, target test metric and factual accuracy checks. Don't prescribe "cut every 2 seconds" or fake psychological causal certainty.
