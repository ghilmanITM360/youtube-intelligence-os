## Core failure

The edit translates nouns into separate stock-like objects and sentences into labels. It seldom translates a claim into an observable consequence. A chair, a stopwatch, a warning triangle and a colored human are assembled like presentation elements. The references more often build an explanatory drawing in which the subject changes, the character reacts, an absurd consequence develops, or a comparison acquires physical meaning.

The human is especially incompatible: filled skin, blue shirt, charcoal trousers, shoes, hair mass and outlined anatomical limbs sit beside almost empty outline furniture. Actual portrait examples use unfilled round heads, single-stroke torsos and limbs, angular elbows/knees and pose-led expression. Removing the blue alone would not correct the anatomy.

Empty space is **not inherently wrong**: R05 and R02 have very sparse states. Here it is unproductive because no new relation develops in that space, and the subject is often too small to carry the meaning. Adding more icons would not solve it.

## Section findings

### 0.000–6.133: introduction and safety hook

**Observed:** A chair begins alone; an empty stopwatch appears, then `30 s`; a small red triangle joins them. The chair changes size/placement around 4.6 s while the other symbols remain. Caption lines sit near the bottom.

**Failure:** A inventory of three nouns replaces the promised tension. Nothing attempts the test; nothing visibly overrides it. The warning floats beside the chair rather than stopping an action. The large top/bottom void does not stage a reveal or establish scale. The stopwatch's precise double circle, tall thin chair, and rounded red icon read as unrelated asset-library families.

**Retain:** Semantic reveal near `30-second` and `safety rule`; a short hold after the rule lets the idea settle. Retain those functions, not the triangle scale animation or layout.

**Replace:** Show the same character preparing to rise, then visibly refusing to continue. Safety changes the scene's behavior.

### 6.133–11.367: setup, timer, counting instruction

**Observed:** The filled blue-shirt person sits in the outline chair for roughly five seconds. A small timer is added. Captions do almost all explanatory work.

**Failure:** The character could come from a workplace training deck. No face is drawn, yet clothing/anatomy are fully modeled; this is not the references' stick construction. The timer is an adjacent noun. The hands lie near the knees; the image does not actively explain unassisted movement. Long hold is not inherently wrong, but this hold supplies no new relation after the timer appears.

**Retain:** Chair fixed as a spatial anchor; timer attached to the procedure; narration continuous.

**Replace:** Registered seated/preparation/standing poses with a simple native time bracket and a count whose appearance is caused by the demonstrated repetition.

### 11.367–14.867: stand and sit

**Observed:** Abrupt seated/standing/seated replacement. Tiny `1` appears while standing; `2` appears after sitting. The numerals sit close to the chair's back.

**Failure:** One demonstrated rise-and-return produces two visible counts. This is a visual explanation error independent of any clinical counting convention: the edit communicates two repetitions without showing two repetitions. The numeral placement tangles with the chair. The large filled standing silhouette dominates the fine chair outline. There is no authored preparation pose.

**Retain:** Discrete pose changes and stable framing are defensible reference devices. Hard swapping is not itself the problem.

**Replace:** One clearly demonstrated action with one illustrative tally. Do not depict standing on the chair despite the script's phrase `stand up on the chair`; show rising from the seat with feet on the floor. No clinical cutoff is introduced.

### 14.867–19.067: unsafe, dizziness, ataxia

**Observed:** Same seated asset persists. Curved marks appear beside the face. A warning icon appears. At 17.533 s the chair's live track changes to a stopwatch while the seated person persists until 19.067 s: the person visibly floats without a seat, with a timer behind the head/body.

**Failure:** The arcs are an attached effect, not a changed physical or perceptual state. Head, arms and torso show no response. Neither dizziness nor lack of coordination is explained. The object substitution breaks scene causality. Reusing the unchanged person here makes the symptom beat visually indistinguishable from setup.

**Retain:** `However` as interruption; stop further repetitions before describing symptoms.

**Replace:** A distinct seated pause pose and a raised stop-hand pose. One hand to the forehead and a changed torso/head relation can signal discomfort without a collapse or diagnostic claim. No wobble loops, stars, dramatic falls or continued exercise. The chair remains present in every seated state.

### 19.067–20.767: do not test

**Observed:** Timer briefly survives; at 19.233 s a blank rounded card replaces it. A detached red cross and `DO NOT TEST` remain, plus the verbal caption.

**Failure:** The physical test vanishes. The cross does not consistently cancel a visible target. A score-shaped panel arrives before the score idea. The instruction is communicated twice as text instead of once as a visible cessation.

**Retain:** Decisive change on the negation and a short hold.

**Replace:** Keep the same seated person and chair, open stop palm, stopped time indicator, no added tally.

### 20.767–24.467: personal count and universal number

**Observed:** `YOUR SCORE` fills the card. Three exact copies of the standing person appear small underneath. `ONE NUMBER FOR EVERYONE` and a red not-equal sign explain the intended relation.

**Failure:** Tiny duplicates are demographic clipart, not three people in explanatory situations. They are the same character and thus undermine the idea of individual context. No number actually moves, fits, fails or gets compared. The image requires the heading and caption to tell viewers what it means. A large empty vertical gap separates the card and figures.

**Retain:** Present a personal result before rejecting universal application; negate only after the false inference becomes intelligible.

**Replace:** A physical record with a symbolic personal count, then an oversized stamp trying to apply that same result to another person's separate record. Two substantial actors interact; no row of icons. Stop the stamp before contact. This is a new metaphor, not a claimed scene from the corpus.

### 24.467–29.133: low score does not diagnose

**Observed:** Score panel above a diagnosis rectangle; down arrow appears; not-equal replaces it. `YOUR SCORE` overlaps the shrinking upper card around 27.6 s.

**Failure:** A flowchart recites the logic rather than dramatizing the invalid transformation. The low-score concern has no visible owner. The empty diagnosis rectangle is meaningful only because of its label. The card changes scale without its text changing coherently. This is presentation UI with hand-drawn borders.

**Retain:** A proposed inference followed by a clear denial. This reveal order is sound.

**Replace:** Reuse the physical stamp as the absurd shortcut. `LOW?` is the character's question, never a normative threshold. A stamp marked `DIAGNOSIS` is held off the paper; the count remains unchanged. The humor targets the shortcut, never the person or symptoms.

### 29.133–34.400: interpretation and channel ending

**Observed:** Score card, `Interpret the score properly`, `Full video on channel`, plus narration captions. The score outline ends at 32.867 s; only text remains through the end.

**Failure:** The story has ended before the narration does. There is no visual payoff, no consequence of the warning against over-interpretation, and no object continuity beyond the heading. Three tiers of writing compete. This is not an earned ending even though some actual references have text-heavy or promotional endings.

**Retain:** The final invitation follows the non-diagnosis message, not an invented diagnosis.

**Replace:** The small result paper opens into a larger physical explanatory drawing; the count becomes one part of a scene shared by the participant and a generic explainer. The rejected stamp rests aside. Finish on that expanded scene with a small selective `Full video on channel` cue.
