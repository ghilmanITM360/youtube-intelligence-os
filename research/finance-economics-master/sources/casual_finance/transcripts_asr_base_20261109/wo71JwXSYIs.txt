Britain went first. A decade ago, long conventional guilt were nearly 30% of the issuance program. This year's plan has them under 10%. The debt office even set it on the record. Market feedback noted continued declining demand for long-dated conventional guilt, in particular from the domestic pension fund sector, which is the polite way of saying, we asked around and nobody wants them. Japan cut its issuance of super long government bonds for the coming year to about 17 trillion yen, the lowest in 17 years, with the Japanese Ministry of Finance now considering reducing issuance even further in the coming months. America's making the move to the short end as well. Short-term Treasury bills now run around 22% of the debt. Above the 15-20% range, the Treasury's own advisory committee recommends. Across the OECD, Treasury bills now out-issue fixed rate bonds entirely. The ratio of 30-year plus debt sold against one to five-year debt is now the lowest since 2008. The OECD's phrase for all of this is that it's cost-efficient X&T, which is Latin for beforehand. So what they're saying is, it's cheaper for now, because shortening doesn't reduce what you owe. It just changes how often you have to ask.
