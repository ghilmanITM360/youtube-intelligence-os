## 2. The eight questions, answered once (applies to all three concepts unless a concept overrides)
1. Central theme: how money and the economy work, shown through one real number at a time.
2. Audience: adults 22 to 45 who feel prices, rent, jobs or interest rates in daily life and want a mechanism, not advice (HYPOTHESIS; no audience data exists).
3. Unique value: one verified number, one visible contradiction, one mechanism, with the source on screen.
4. Questions we answer: "why is X like this?" with price, rent, wages, fares, rates, company economics. Not "what should I buy".
5. Format identity: analytical-educational with story structure (not documentary, not news read-out, not advice).
6. Storytelling angle: open with a dated, sourced fact, state the contradiction, resolve with a mechanism (matches Casual Finance and Crayon openings, Phase B).
7. Competitive advantage we can actually control: primary-source verification and a visible source trail; own visual system (no round-head cartoon, no animal cast); named human or clearly labelled narration policy.
8. Difference from references: they sell speed (Casual Finance, Paper Billions) or characters (Monkey Economics, Crayon). We sell verification and a clear mechanism. HYPOTHESIS: viewers reward that; untested.

## 3. Three positioning concepts

### Concept 1: "The number behind the price" (data-led mechanism explainer)
| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Positioning statement | We take one price people complain about (rent, airfares, groceries) and show who actually sets it, using the official number. |
| Target audience | Adults who feel cost-of-living pressure and are suspicious of headlines |
| Content pillars | (1) Prices and who sets them (rent, fares, food); (2) How official statistics are built (CPI, jobs); (3) "Two numbers that disagree" |
| Visual identity direction | Real charts with date and source on screen, flat paper-and-ink look, one accent colour, no mascot (decided in branding phase) |
| Storytelling identity | Fact, contradiction, mechanism, what it means (not what to buy) |
| Differentiation | Source-on-screen as a rule; only reference doing something close is Casual Finance, which is news-speed and higher volume |
| Advantages | Fits both live topics (rent, airfares); verifiable; durable evergreen core; low look-alike risk; works for Shorts clips |
| Disadvantages | Cost-of-living/housing/rates are broad with small-channel medians 2.2K to 4K (Phase C); data charts are labour-heavy; abstract if not tied to a story |
| Long-term sustainability | High: new CPI and data releases monthly give a standing hook (HYPOTHESIS); needs a data pipeline we do not have |

### Concept 2: "How one industry actually makes money" (mechanism-of-industry explainer)
| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Positioning statement | We open one industry (airline, port, shipping, data centre, grocery) and trace where each dollar goes. |
| Target audience | Curious generalists plus business-minded viewers; strong overlap with Neu/Brimm. audiences (INFERRED) |
| Content pillars | (1) Follow the dollar through one ticket/product; (2) Hidden fixed costs; (3) Why the industry looks like this |
| Visual identity direction | Cutaway diagrams and maps, objects as the visual hero, one running "ledger" device |
| Storytelling identity | One object, one price, split into slices (hook model H2 in Phase E) |
| Differentiation | Few competitors (10 channels in the bucket); none of the primary six covers it |
| Advantages | Highest small-channel median in our data (37K, VERIFIED count); airfare topic already fits; strong visuals; evergreen |
| Disadvantages | Evidence rests on 2 channels (HYPOTHESIS); generic "how airlines make money" was REJECTED in Phase D (no gap, existing videos); needs sourcing per video; the "Economics of owning X" template is a mass-production trap |
| Long-term sustainability | Medium: a finite list of industries, but each can be reopened with news; risk of a templated feel |

### Concept 3: "The story with a mechanism" (company and history explainer, own look)
| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Positioning statement | We tell one company or crisis as a story and end on the economic rule it proves. |
| Target audience | Story-first viewers 18 to 40, broad |
| Content pillars | (1) Company rise and fall; (2) Crises and bubbles; (3) "The rule behind the story" |
| Visual identity direction | Own illustrated style (not round-head cartoons), archive documents and charts |
| Storytelling identity | Dated scene, stakes, turn, mechanism |
| Differentiation | Weakest: Crayon (319K), Capital Case, Paper Billions already own it with similar look |
| Advantages | Proven ceiling (Crayon 2.8M to 4.2M top videos); Capital Case shows small-channel viability (12K subs, 54K median) |
| Disadvantages | Most look-alike risk; AI/Nvidia stories crowded in the same weeks (Phase B); heavy research per video; ours would be late and smaller |
| Long-term sustainability | High topic supply, but weak ownership of any position |

## 5. Recommendation
**Concept 1 as the identity, with Concept 2 as the main series inside it.** One-line positioning (working): "One price, one official number, one mechanism: why things cost what they cost."
- Concept 1 is the channel promise (verified numbers, source on screen). Concept 2 is the repeatable video type that proves the promise (follow one ticket, one rent, one product).
- Rent and airfares already fit this blend. Airfares is time-boxed news; rent is durable.
- Reject Concept 3 as a core: late, look-alike risk, and the three leaders are stronger.
- Keep "who is on screen" (human or labelled narration) as a decision for the owner; no AI persona as adviser.

Why not only Concept 2: its evidence rests on two channels and the generic version was rejected; inside Concept 1 it gets a stronger frame.
