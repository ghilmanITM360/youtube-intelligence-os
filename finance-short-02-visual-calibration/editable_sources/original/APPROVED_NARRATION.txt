The Federal Reserve raised interest rates. And its own interest bill went up.

In September, the Fed raised rates by a quarter point. Here's the problem: banks keep roughly three trillion dollars in reserves at the Fed, and the Fed pays them interest.

Just that quarter-point increase would add about seven and a half billion dollars a year in interest costs, if reserves stayed the same.

But much of the Fed's bond income doesn't rise as quickly.

When expenses outweigh income, losses build into what's called a deferred asset. By early October, that balance was roughly two hundred and thirty-four billion dollars.

Until future earnings cover it, the Fed's normal payments to the Treasury stay paused. In twenty twenty-one, those payments were more than one hundred and seven billion dollars.

Higher rates can fight inflation. But they can also make the Fed's own finances harder to repair.
