Exercise number five. Seated toe and
ankle wake up.
This is where we begin, and I want you
to understand why we start here before
anything else.
When numbness and tingling affect the
legs and feet after 70, the symptoms
almost always start from the bottom up.
The toes go first, then the ball of the
foot, then the ankle. Then it creeps
upward.
That pattern tells us something very
important about where the problem begins
and where the solution needs to start as
well.
Your toes and ankles are the lowest
points in your entire circulatory
system. When you sit for long periods
watching television, reading, or riding
in a car, blood has a natural tendency
to pool in these areas. At the same
time, the small muscles inside your feet
and around your ankles become inactive.
When both of those things happen
together, the nerves in your feet begin
to receive less oxygen-rich blood. And
when nerves are deprived of the blood
flow they need, they send distress
signals. Those signals feel like
tingling, buzzing, numbness, or that
strange sensation people often describe
as their foot falling asleep. Except
after 70, it starts happening more often
and taking longer to resolve.
This first exercise directly targets
that problem. It is done entirely in a
chair, which makes it safe for virtually
everyone, regardless of joint pain or
balance concerns.
Here is how to do it. Sit upright in a
sturdy chair with both feet flat on the
floor. Begin by slowly curling your toes
downward as if you were trying to grip
the floor through your shoes. Hold that
position for 3 seconds, then gently
release. You should feel a mild
activation in the arch of your foot and
the base of your toes. That sensation is
exactly what we want. It means the
muscles are waking up.
After 10 repetitions of the toe curl,
move into the ankle circles. Lift one
foot just a few inches off the floor and
slowly rotate your ankle in a wide,
smooth circle. Five times clockwise,
then five times counterclockwise.
Take your time. The circle should be
large and deliberate, not rushed. Think
of it as drawing a slow, careful circle
in the air with your big toe. Then
switch to the other foot and repeat.
Research noted by the American Heart
Association has found that gentle foot
and ankle
during extended periods of sitting helps
reduce fluid build-up in the lower
extremities and supports healthy venous
return.
These are not dramatic movements, but
their effect on circulation at the foot
level is real and meaningful.
A common mistake is making the ankle
circles too small and too fast. Smaller,
quicker movements recruit very little of
the surrounding tissue. What you want is
a slow, broad circle that gently engages
the muscles, tendons, and connective
tissue around the entire ankle joint.
Another mistake is tensing the rest of
your body while doing this. Keep your
shoulders relaxed, your breathing easy,
and your focus on how your feet feel as
you move them.
For older adults dealing with numbness
and tingling, this exercise is the
foundation. It sends a clear signal to
the lowest part of your nervous system
that circulation is beginning to move
again.
Done consistently, many people notice
within a few weeks that the morning
numbness begins to ease and that the
pins and needles feeling at night
becomes less frequent and less intense.
Exercise number four, ball foot massage
and nerve activation. Once the ankles
and toes have been gently reawakened,
the next step is to go deeper into the
foot itself, into the muscles, pressure
points, and nerve pathways that lie
beneath the surface of the sole.
For this exercise, you will need one
firm ball. A tennis ball works well. A
slightly harder rubber ball works even
better. You will keep this exercise
seated as well, which keeps it
completely safe and accessible.
Here is why this step matters so much.
The sole of your foot contains an
incredibly dense network of nerve
endings. In fact, there are more sensory
nerve endings per square centimeter in
the bottom of your foot than almost
anywhere else in the body. When
circulation is poor and muscles are
inactive, these nerve endings become
compressed and under activated. The
result is that dull, muffled feeling,
the sense that your feet are not quite
connected to the rest of you.
By applying gentle rolling pressure to
the sole of the foot, you stimulate
blood flow directly into the small
capillaries that feed these nerve
endings. You also begin to reactivate
the intrinsic muscles of the foot, the
small muscles inside the foot itself
that most people never consciously
exercise.
To practice this, place the ball under
one foot while seated. Rest your foot on
top of it with gentle but firm pressure.
Not so light that you barely feel it and
not so hard that it causes pain. Begin
rolling the ball slowly forward toward
your toes, then backward toward your
heel. Then roll it from the inner edge
of your foot toward the outer edge. Then
try slow clockwise and counterclockwise
circles.
As you do this, pay close attention to
any spots that feel more tender or
sensitive than others. These tender
points are almost always areas where
circulation has been most restricted.
When you find one, pause on it. Apply
steady, gentle pressure for 10 to 15
seconds, then continue rolling. Over
several days of consistent practice,
patients consistently report that those
tender spots become less sensitive, a
clear sign that blood flow is improving
in those areas.
After the rolling massage, try this
additional movement. Position the ball
under your toes and attempt to grip it
with your toes, pressing down as if
trying to hold the ball in place.
This is surprisingly difficult for most
older adults, and that difficulty itself
reveals how much the intrinsic foot
muscles have weakened.
Even the attempt, even if you cannot
fully grip the ball, sends a powerful
reactivation signal to those muscles.
Do this exercise for 5 to 10 minutes on
each foot. Make sure you do both feet
every time, even if only one foot is
symptomatic. The body functions best in
balance, and working only one side can
create asymmetries over time.
A common mistake is rushing the ball
movements. Speed removes the therapeutic
benefit entirely. Slow, deliberate
pressure is what drives blood into the
small vessels and reactivates the nerve
pathways.
Exercise number three. Supported
heel-to-toe weight rock.
After the feet and ankles have been
reactivated in a seated position, it is
time to gently introduce the body to
standing, but in a way that feels safe,
supported, and completely within your
control.
This exercise is done standing behind a
sturdy chair or near a kitchen counter.
You keep both hands lightly resting on
the support at all times. Your feet stay
hip-width apart, flat on the floor.
The movement itself is simple. You
slowly shift your weight forward onto
the balls of your feet and your toes.
Not quite a full heel raise, but a
gentle forward lean through the feet.
Hold that for 2 or 3 seconds, feeling
the pressure shift toward the front of
your foot. Then slowly rock your weight
back onto your heels, lifting the front
of your feet just slightly off the
floor. Hold again, then return to
center.
The rhythm should be slow and flowing.
Forward, center, back, center. Like a
gentle rocking motion, like the tide
moving in and out.
In everyday life, every single step you
take requires this kind of weight
transfer. When the muscles responsible
for this movement become weak or poorly
coordinated, walking starts to feel
uncertain. People begin to shuffle
rather than stride. They hesitate before
stepping. That hesitation, over time,
reduces activity, which further weakens
circulation and makes the numbness
worse.
Guidance from the Mayo Clinic emphasizes
that supported standing movements
improve lower limb circulation and
neuromuscular coordination in older
adults, particularly when balance
confidence has declined.
This gentle rocking pattern retrains the
feet, ankles, and lower legs to share
and transfer weight efficiently, which
is one of the most fundamental
requirements for healthy circulation
during movement.
A common mistake is locking the knees.
When the knees are rigid, the body
cannot distribute weight smoothly, and
the calves, which are critical for
pushing blood upward, cannot activate
properly. Keep a very slight natural
softness in the knees throughout this
movement.
Another mistake is leaning the upper
body too far forward or too far back to
compensate.
The movement should come from the feet
and ankles, not from bending at the
waist. Keep your chest open, your gaze
forward, and your core gently engaged.
For older adults experiencing numbness
and tingling, this exercise begins to
reconnect the nervous system's awareness
of where the feet are in space, a sense
called proprioception that often
diminishes alongside circulation
problems. Rebuilding it reduces the risk
of stumbling, increases confidence while
walking, and helps the legs begin to
feel more present and reliable again.
Exercise number two. Towel toe curl in a
lying position.
This exercise moves to the floor, or to
your bed if getting down to the floor is
not comfortable for you. It is done
lying on your back, and it specifically
targets the muscles of the toes and the
arch of the foot in a way that no
standing exercise can fully replicate.
Here is why this position matters. When
you are lying down, there is no
gravitational pressure on the feet and
no balance requirement. This allows you
to focus all of your attention and
effort on the small muscles of the toes
and foot arch. The muscles that are most
directly connected to the nerve pathways
that produce numbness and tingling.
To practice this, lie comfortably on
your back with your legs extended. Place
a small towel folded flat under the toes
of one foot so that your toes rest on
top of it. Now, using only your toes,
try to curl them downward and grip the
towel as if you are trying to scrunch it
up toward you.
Pull with your toes. Hold the grip for 5
seconds, then release completely. Repeat
this 10 to 15 times on each foot.
This movement is harder than it looks.
Most people over 70 discover very
quickly that their toes have almost no
gripping strength remaining. This
weakness in the intrinsic toe muscles
correlates strongly with the degree of
numbness a patient experiences.
Because these muscles play a direct role
in driving microcirculation
through the smallest blood vessels in
the foot.
When you begin this exercise, do not be
discouraged if your toes barely move.
Even the neurological signal, the
intention to grip, begins to retrain the
nerve pathways.
Within a few days of daily practice,
most people notice measurably more
movement and control.
After the towel curl, extend both legs
flat and perform the final part of this
lying sequence. Slowly rotate both feet
outward pointing your toes away from
each other. Hold for 3 seconds and feel
the stretch along your shins and the
outer edges of your feet. Then rotate
both feet inward crossing your toes
toward each other.
Hold again. This gentle rotation
stretches the nerve tissue that runs
along the length of the lower leg,
including the peroneal nerve, which is
one of the most commonly compressed
nerves in people who experience lower
leg numbness.
A common mistake is using the entire leg
to perform the towel curl instead of
isolating the movement to the toes. If
you feel your thigh or calf working
hard, reduce the effort and focus more
specifically on the toes themselves.
Exercise number one, controlled heel
raise and nerve stretch flow. This is
the exercise I recommend most
consistently to patients who come to me
with ongoing numbness and tingling in
their legs and feet.
Not because it is the most demanding, it
is not, but because it combines
everything the legs need in a single
flowing movement. It activates the calf
muscles, supports venous blood return,
stretches the nerve pathways along the
back of the leg, and builds functional
standing strength all at the same time.
This exercise is done standing behind a
chair with both hands resting lightly on
the back for support. Feet are hip-width
apart.
Begin by very slowly rising on to the
balls of your feet, a full heel raise.
Take 3 full seconds to rise. At the top,
pause and hold for 2 seconds. Feel the
calves engage. Feel the pressure through
the balls of your feet. Then lower your
heels back down just as slowly.
3 seconds on the way down. Do not drop.
Control the descent completely.
Once both heels are back on the floor,
add the next part of the flow. Gently
bend both knees just slightly. Not a
squat, not even close. Just a small soft
bend, then straighten again.
This brief knee bend recruits the
quadriceps and hamstrings into the
movement, which creates a coordinated
muscle contraction across the entire
lower leg. Exactly the kind of combined
activation that research from the
National Institutes of Health has shown
to improve venous return and functional
mobility in older adults more
effectively than isolated single muscle
exercises.
After the knee bend, finish the flow
with a gentle foot rotation. Toes out,
hold, toes in, hold, return to center.
This keeps the nerve tissue mobile and
prevents the stiffness that contributes
to nighttime tingling.
One full repetition takes approximately
12 to 15 seconds when done properly.
Start with eight to 10 repetitions. Over
time, work toward 15.
The goal is never speed. The goal is
deliberate controlled movement that the
nervous system can learn from and
repeat. A common mistake is turning this
exercise into something more aggressive,
bouncing on the toes, squatting too
deep, or rushing through the sequence.
This is not a strength workout. It is a
circulation and nerve activation
routine, and its power lies entirely in
the slowness and control with which you
perform it.
For older adults dealing with numbness
and tingling, this exercise, when
practiced daily, tends to produce the
most noticeable results.
Many patients report that within 3 to 4
weeks of consistent daily repetition,
the nighttime tingling begins to reduce.
The feet begin to feel more present.
Walking feels more grounded and
confident.
Bonus: The Vitamin Connection Your
Nerves Depend On.
Before we close, there is one more thing
I want to share because movement alone
will only take you so far if your nerves
are lacking two critical nutrients.
Over the course of my career, I have
seen countless older adults do
everything right with exercise and still
struggle with persistent numbness. And
in many of those cases, the missing
piece was found in a simple blood test.
The first nutrient is vitamin B12.
This vitamin is essential for the
protective myelin sheath that surrounds
every nerve in your body. When B12
levels fall too low, that sheath begins
to deteriorate and the result is exactly
the kind of numbness, tingling, and
burning sensation we have been
discussing throughout this video.
B12 levels should ideally sit close to
350 or above. If yours are lower than
that, speak with your doctor about
supplementation.
The second is vitamin D3.
Beyond its well-known role in bone
health, D3 plays a meaningful role in
nerve function and muscle performance. A
general recommendation is to maintain D3
levels between 70 and 80. If your levels
are sitting at 30 or 40, that alone can
contribute to the weakness and
discomfort you feel in your legs and
feet.
Ask your doctor to check both of these
at your next visit. These are not
expensive tests and the results can
completely change the direction of your
recovery.
