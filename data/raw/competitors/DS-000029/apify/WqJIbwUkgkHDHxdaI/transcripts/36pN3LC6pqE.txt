Why mornings are the most dangerous time
of day.
Let me explain what's actually happening
in your body when you wake up. Because
once you understand this, you'll never
rush out of bed again.
When you've been lying still for 6, 7,
or 8 hours, several things happen
simultaneously.
First, your muscles cool down and
stiffen. Just like a car engine that's
been sitting overnight in cold weather,
your muscles need time to warm up before
they're ready to perform. When you jump
out of bed without warming up, you're
asking cold, stiff muscles to
immediately take on your full body
weight, manage your balance, and respond
to any unexpected movement.
That's a recipe for trouble.
Second, your blood pressure drops. When
you're lying flat, your heart doesn't
have to work very hard to circulate
blood throughout your body.
The moment you sit up, especially
quickly, your heart suddenly has to pump
blood upward against gravity.
For many people over 70, this adjustment
doesn't happen fast enough. The result
is a condition called orthostatic
hypotension, a sudden drop in blood
pressure that causes dizziness,
light-headedness, and in some cases, a
complete loss of consciousness. This is
one of the leading causes of morning
falls, and most people have no idea it's
happening to them.
Third, your balance system is sluggish.
Your brain uses three systems to
maintain balance. Your eyes, your inner
ear, and the sensory receptors in your
muscles and joints.
After hours of stillness and sleep, all
three of these systems need time to
recalibrate.
When you stand up too quickly, you're
asking your balance system to perform at
full capacity before it's even fully
awake.
Now, here's the critical point. All
three of these problems can be addressed
before you ever leave the bed. That's
the whole purpose of this routine. By
the time you finish these five
exercises, your muscles will be warm,
your circulation will be adjusted, and
your balance system will be fully
online. You'll stand up safer, steadier,
and more confident every single morning.
Exercise one.
Ankle pumps and circles.
Why it matters.
This is where we start. And the reason
is simple. Your ankles and calves are
your body's circulation pump. Every time
your calf muscles contract, they squeeze
the veins in your lower legs and push
blood back up towards your heart. After
hours of lying still, blood naturally
pools in your lower extremities. Ankle
pumps get that circulation moving again,
which helps your blood pressure adjust
before you sit up. They also begin
warming up the ankle joints that are
essential for balance and safe stepping.
How to do it.
Lying flat on your back with your legs
straight, flex both feet towards you.
Pulling your toes up toward the ceiling
as far as comfortably possible. Hold for
2 seconds, then point your feet away
from you by extending through the toes
as far as comfortable.
Hold for 2 seconds. That's one rep.
Do 15 repetitions of this pump motion.
Then, keeping your legs still, slowly
rotate both feet in large circles. 10
circles clockwise, then 10 circles
counter-clockwise.
Move slowly and with full range of
motion. You should feel gentle warmth
building in your calves and ankles.
What to focus on. Keep your legs relaxed
on the bed.
The movement is coming from the ankle
and foot only. Breathe normally
throughout.
Exercise two, knee-to-chest gentle
pulls. Why it matters. After a full
night of sleep, your lower back, hips,
and hip flexors are compressed and
tight. These are the exact muscles you
need to lift your legs, stabilize your
pelvis, and control your movement as you
stand up. This gentle stretch releases
that tightness before you ask those
muscles to work, reducing stiffness,
improving circulation to the hip area,
and waking up the sensory receptors in
your joints that feed information to
your balance system.
How to do it.
Still lying on your back, bend your
right knee and bring it slowly towards
your chest. Reach both hands behind your
right thigh, not the knee, and gently
pull your leg closer to your body until
you feel a comfortable stretch in your
lower back, hip, and buttock.
Hold for 20 to 30 seconds. Breathe
slowly and deeply. Release slowly, lower
your right leg back to the bed, and
repeat on the left side.
Do two repetitions on each side.
What to focus on?
Never pull by the knee joint itself.
Always hold behind the thigh. Keep your
opposite leg as relaxed as possible. If
you cannot reach behind your thigh
comfortably, use a towel or belt loop
behind your leg instead. The stretch
should feel relieving, never painful.
Exercise three. Supine marching.
Why it matters.
This exercise is a direct bridge between
the bed and your morning routine. Supine
marching, marching while lying on your
back, wakes up your hip flexors,
activates your core, and begins training
the exact movement pattern you use every
time you take a step. It also continues
improving circulation and starts sending
strong signals from your legs to your
brain, essentially booting up the
communication system that your balance
depends on.
How to do it.
Lying on your back with your knees bent
and feet flat on the bed, slowly lift
your right foot a few inches off the
mattress, just enough to feel your hip
flexor engage. Hold for 2 seconds. Lower
slowly, then lift the left foot in the
same way. Hold for 2 seconds.
Lower slowly.
That's one full repetition. Do 10
repetitions on each side, 20 lifts
total.
Move at a slow, deliberate pace. Think
of this as marching in slow motion. Each
lift should be intentional and
controlled.
What to focus on? Keep your lower back
gently pressed into the mattress. Don't
let it arch up.
Keep your core lightly engaged
throughout. If lifting your foot off the
mattress is uncomfortable, you can
modify this by simply pressing one heel
firmly into the mattress while the other
lifts slightly. You'll still get the
muscle activation you need.
Progress tip: As this becomes easier,
try holding each lift for 5 seconds
instead of two. This significantly
increases the hip flexor and core
challenge.
Exercise four: Seated ankle taps.
Why it matters:
This is the transition exercise, the one
that moves you from lying down to
sitting upright. Sitting on the edge of
the bed is itself a significant postural
change, and this exercise gives your
body time to adjust to that new position
while also continuing to strengthen your
ankles, activate your core, and train
the dynamic balance you need for
walking. Think of this as the final
systems check before standing up.
How to do it:
Slowly bring yourself to a seated
position on the edge of the bed. Take 10
full seconds to do this. Don't rush.
Plant both feet flat on the floor,
hip-width apart. Sit up straight, hands
resting gently on your thighs.
Now, keeping your heels on the floor,
lift the toes of your right foot up
toward the ceiling as high as
comfortable.
Tap them back down. Then lift the toes
of your left foot. Tap them back down.
That's one rep.
Do 15 repetitions on each side,
alternating feet.
Then reverse. Keeping your toes on the
floor, lift your right heel up, then tap
it down, then the left heel.
15 reps each side.
What to focus on: Sit upright
throughout. Don't slouch or lean
forward. Keep your breathing slow and
steady. If you feel any dizziness during
the sitting phase, stop the tapping and
simply focus on slow, deep breaths until
the dizziness passes. If it doesn't pass
within 30 seconds, lie back down and
call for assistance.
Progress tip. Once this feels
comfortable, try doing the taps with
your eyes closed. This forces your
balance system to rely entirely on the
sensory information from your feet and
ankles. Exactly the training your brain
needs to handle real-world balance
challenges.
Exercise five. Seated shoulder rolls and
deep breathing.
Why it matters.
This final exercise serves two purposes
that most people wouldn't expect from a
fall prevention routine.
First, your shoulders, neck, and upper
back are often deeply tense after sleep,
and that tension affects your posture.
Poor posture when you stand up means
your head is tilted forward, your center
of gravity is shifted, and your balance
is compromised from the very first step.
Second, deep diaphragmatic breathing
directly stimulates your vagus nerve,
which helps regulate blood pressure.
Three to five slow, deep breaths taken
while sitting upright can meaningfully
reduce the risk of that blood pressure
drop that causes dizziness.
How to do it.
Still seated on the edge of the bed,
roll your shoulders slowly backward, up,
back, down, and around in a large,
relaxed circle. Do five slow rolls
backward, then five slow rolls forward.
Let your neck relax. Let your jaw
soften.
Then place one hand on your chest and
one hand on your belly. Take a slow,
deep breath in through your nose for
four counts, feeling your belly push out
against your hand, not your chest rise.
Hold gently for two counts, then exhale
slowly through your mouth for six
counts, feeling your belly fall.
Do five of these slow, deep breaths.
By the time you finish, your posture
will be taller, your muscles will be
warm, and your cardiovascular system
will be prepared for the transition to
standing.
What to focus on?
Don't rush the breathing.
This is not just relaxation, it is
physiological preparation. Each breath
is actively helping your body regulate.
Let it work.
The final step, the 30-second pause.
This is not an exercise, but it may be
the most important part of the entire
routine.
After you finish exercise five, before
you stand up, simply sit on the edge of
the bed for 30 full seconds. Feet flat
on the floor, back straight, breathing
normally.
This pause gives your blood pressure one
final window to fully adjust to the
upright position. It lets your inner ear
recalibrate. It lets your eyes adjust to
the light, and it removes the single
most dangerous element of the morning
routine, rushing.
When you do stand, push through your
heels, not your toes. Stand tall
immediately. Don't lean forward. And if
there is a nightstand, bed rail, or wall
within reach, have your hand near it for
the first few seconds. Not because you
expect to fall, but because that safety
net gives your nervous system the
confidence it needs to perform its best.
