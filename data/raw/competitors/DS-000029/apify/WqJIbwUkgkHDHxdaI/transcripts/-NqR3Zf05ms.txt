Why do adults over 70 trip more often?
Every step requires your foot to swing
forward and clear the ground, and two
muscle groups must activate at the right
time.
The first group is the ankle
dorsiflexors along the front of your
shin. They lift your toes in the front
of your foot as your leg swings. When
they weaken, your toes may drag and
catch a rug, threshold, or sidewalk.
The second group is your hip flexors at
the front of the hip. They drive your
knee forward and help lift the leg. When
they become weak, your foot stays closer
to the floor, forcing the ankle muscles
to compensate.
Imagine opening a door with a stiff
hinge. It moves, but not far enough. The
same thing happens when these muscles
lose strength. Your foot does not rise
high enough. Fortunately, both groups
can become stronger with consistent
exercise.
Exercise one, the stair activation.
Why it matters.
This is the foundation of everything.
The single most direct exercise you can
do to target both muscle groups
responsible for tripping at the exact
same time.
Most exercises work one muscle group at
a time. The step lift is different. It
combines an ankle movement and a hip
movement into one fluid two-stage
repetition, which means every single rep
you complete is training the precise
sequence of muscle activations that your
body needs to execute with every step
you take.
What makes this exercise so powerful for
seniors over 70 is not just what it
strengthens, it is what it retrains.
After months or years of shuffling
dragging steps, the neural pathway that
tells your ankle to lift and your hip to
drive has grown faint from disuse.
The step lift sends a clear strong
signal along that exact pathway with
every repetition. Over time, the signal
gets stronger, the muscles respond
faster, and your foot begins to clear
the ground the way it was always
designed to.
How to do it.
All you need is the bottom step of your
staircase or a low sturdy footstool.
Stand tall near the wall or handrail
with one hand available for support at
all times. Place the foot of your
working leg on the step, heel resting on
the step, toes pointing upward. Your
standing leg should be slightly bent,
never locked. Back straight, chin level,
shoulders relaxed.
Now, lift your toes as high as you
possibly can toward your shin, keeping
your heel firmly on the step. Hold for 1
second. Feel the front of your shin
engage. Then, while keeping those toes
lifted, slowly raise your knee upward
toward your chest as high as you
comfortably can.
Hold for 1 second at the top, then lower
your knee slowly, lower your toes, and
return to the starting position. That is
one complete repetition.
Do 10 repetitions on each leg. Complete
two sets per session. Rest 30 seconds
between sets.
What to focus on?
After several repetitions, you should
feel a mild fatigue building along the
front of your shin and at the front of
your hip. That is exactly right. That is
the signal that these muscles are being
challenged and will grow stronger. Do
not rush the lowering phase. The
controlled descent builds the eccentric
strength your muscles need to stabilize
your foot when it lands on the ground
with each real step.
Progress tip.
Once 10 repetitions feel manageable,
increase to 12, then 15 over the
following weeks. The goal is steady
progressive challenge, not speed, not
strain. Slow and controlled always wins.

Exercise two, the shin power walk.
Why it matters.
The step lift builds the strength. The
toe raise walk teaches your body to use
that strength while actually moving,
which is when it matters most. There is
a critical difference between being able
to lift your toes while standing still
and being able to lift them
automatically, repeatedly, and with full
control while your body is in motion.
This exercise bridges that gap. It
trains your ankle dorsiflexors to fire
consistently with every step, building
the habit of proper foot clearance into
your actual walking pattern rather than
just into a stationary exercise.
Many seniors over 70 who trip regularly
have no difficulty lifting their toes
when they are standing still and paying
full attention.
The problem occurs when they are
walking, thinking about something else,
carrying something, or simply moving
through their day on autopilot.
The toe raise walk trains the reflex to
fire automatically without conscious
thought, which is exactly what you need
it to do in a real tripping situation.
How to do it?
Find a clear hallway or open space along
a wall. Stand at one end with one hand
lightly touching the wall for support if
needed.
Begin walking, but with each step before
your foot swings forward, consciously
lift your toes upward as high as you can
and keep them lifted throughout the
entire swing phase until your heel makes
contact with the ground. Heel touches
down first, toes still lifted, then the
rest of the foot follows. Take 10 steps
forward this way, turn carefully using
small controlled steps, and walk back 10
steps. That is one set. Complete three
sets with a 30-second rest between each
one.
What to focus on?
Keep your gaze forward, not down at your
feet. In real life, your ankle needs to
lift automatically while your eyes are
focused on where you are going, not on
the mechanics of your own feet. This
exercise begins building that automatic
quality from the very first step. If you
feel unsteady, slow down and keep your
hand on the wall. Speed is never the
goal here.
Progress tip.
Once this feels natural and automatic on
flat ground, try the same walk on a
slightly different surface, from
hardwood to carpet, for example.
The slight variation in texture and
resistance forces your nervous system to
adapt and generalize the pattern, which
is precisely what you need for
real-world safety, where surfaces are
rarely perfectly uniform.
Exercise three, the hip drive march.
Why it matters.
If the ankle dorsiflexors are one half
of the tripping equation, the hip
flexors are the other, and this exercise
targets them directly. When your hip
flexors are strong, they generate the
forward drive that lifts your entire
thigh with each step. That thigh lift is
what creates the chain reaction. Thigh
rises, knee comes up, ankle clears the
ground. When the hip flexors are weak,
that chain reaction breaks down at the
very first link, and no amount of ankle
strength can fully compensate for a hip
that is not doing its share of the work.
The high knee march rebuilds this
lifting reflex from the ground up. It is
simple, it requires no equipment, and it
directly mimics the hip movement your
body needs with every single walking
step. Done consistently, it restores the
power and speed of that hip drive, which
means your foot spends more time safely
in the air and less time dangerously
close to the ground where tripping
happens.
How to do it.
Stand behind a sturdy chair with both
hands resting lightly on the back for
support. Stand tall, back straight, chin
level, shoulders relaxed. Slowly lift
your right knee upward towards your
chest as high as you comfortably can
without leaning your upper body
backward. Hold for two full seconds at
the top, then lower your foot back to
the floor slowly and with complete
control.
Repeat on the left side.
That is one full repetition. Complete 15
repetitions on each side, 30 total lifts
at a slow, deliberate pace throughout.
What to focus on.
The lifting work should come entirely
from your hip, not from momentum, not
from leaning back, not from a quick
swing of the leg. Slow, intentional,
powerful. And the lowering phase matters
just as much as the lift. Do not let
your foot drop. Control the descent all
the way to the floor. This eccentric
control is what stabilizes your foot the
moment it returns to the ground in real
walking. And it is one of the most
under-trained qualities in seniors over
70
Progress tip.
As this becomes easier, practice the
march while moving slowly forward
through your hallway. Lifting each knee
deliberately before stepping, then
placing your heel down first. You're now
combining this exercise directly with
the toe raise walk from exercise two,
creating the closest possible simulation
of proper safe walking mechanics that
you can train at home.

Exercise four, the shin endurance walk.
This exercise targets the ankle
dorsiflexors in the most direct and
concentrated way possible by forcing
them to stay fully activated throughout
an entire period of walking, rather than
just for a single step or a single
repetition.
Walking on your heels requires your shin
muscles to hold your toes lifted
continuously, which builds both the
strength and the endurance of these
muscles in a way that no other exercise
in this routine can replicate.
Endurance matters because in real life,
you are not just taking one step. You
are walking across the grocery lot,
through the grocery store, down the
hallway to the bathroom at 2:00 in the
morning.
Your ankle dorsiflexors need to be
strong enough to do their job not just
once, but repeatedly throughout your
entire day.
How to do it?
Stand near a wall with one hand lightly
touching it for balance. Lift the front
of both feet off the ground
simultaneously so that only your heels
are in contact with the floor. Hold your
toes as high as you can, both feet at
the same time.
Now, begin walking forward on your heels
only, taking small careful steps while
keeping your toes lifted throughout.
Walk 10 steps forward, then carefully
turn and walk 10 steps back.
That is one set. Complete two sets with
a 30-second rest between them.
What to focus on? This will feel
challenging and that is exactly right.
Your shin muscles will fatigue quickly
in the beginning and that is the whole
point. Stay near the wall at all times.
Take very small steps. Do not try to
walk fast or cover distance. The goal is
continuous activation of the
dorsiflexors, not speed or distance. If
your toes drop at any point, stop,
reset, and begin again.
Partial activation does not produce the
same training effect.
Progress tip: Begin with just five steps
in each direction if 10 feels like too
much. Add one or two steps per session
as your endurance builds. Most people
are surprised by how quickly their shin
muscles strengthen with this exercise
because they are muscles that respond
very rapidly to the right kind of direct
challenge.
Exercise five: The clearance step.
Everything you have practiced in the
previous four exercises has been
building toward this moment because this
exercise is the closest real-world
simulation of the exact situation that
causes tripping in seniors over 70.
A rug edge,
a door threshold, a pet toy on the
floor, a garden hose across the path.
In every one of these situations, your
brain needs to detect the obstacle, send
the signal to lift your foot high enough
to clear it, and land safely and stably
on the other side, all in a fraction of
a second.
When that chain of events works
correctly, you step over and keep moving
without breaking stride. When it breaks
down anywhere along the way, when the
signal is slow, when the hip flexor is
too weak to generate the lift, when the
ankle fails to hold the toes high
enough, that is when a stumble becomes a
fall.
This exercise trains every single link
in that chain simultaneously. The
detection, the lift, the clearance, and
the controlled landing. Done
consistently, it builds the automatic
protective reflexes that catch you
before you even realize you are starting
to fall.
How to do it?
Place a pool noodle, a rolled-up towel,
or a similarly soft and lightweight
object on the floor of your hallway. It
should be approximately 4 to 6 in high,
significant enough to require a genuine
deliberate lift, but soft enough that
stepping on it carries absolutely no
risk of injury. Stand a few feet back
from the obstacle with a wall or sturdy
chair within immediate reach.
Walk toward the obstacle at a normal
controlled pace. As you approach, slow
down slightly, lift your right foot
deliberately and early, well before you
reach the object, and step over it with
clear, confident clearance. Then lift
your left foot and step completely past
the obstacle. Continue walking three or
four steps beyond it, turn carefully,
and return to step over it again.
Complete five passes in each direction.
10 total step-overs per session.
What to focus on.
Do not rush as you approach the
obstacle.
The most dangerous moment in real-life
tripping situations is exactly that
split second when you see the hazard
ahead and either rush to clear it or
hesitate at the wrong moment. This
exercise teaches you to slow down,
assess calmly, and execute with full
control. Lift your foot early, earlier
than feels necessary. Give yourself more
clearance than the obstacle seems to
require. That generous margin of safety
is the habit you are building. The habit
that will protect you when a real
obstacle appears unexpectedly on a real
floor in your real life.
Progress tip. Once five passes feel
genuinely comfortable and automatic, try
varying the height slightly. A little
higher one week, a rolled towel of
different thickness the next. You can
also practice while carrying something
light in one hand, such as a small water
bottle. This is a much more accurate
simulation of the way real-life trips
tend to happen, because in real life
your attention is rarely fully on the
floor when an obstacle appears.
Your daily routine.
Here is how to fit all five exercises
into your real life, simply and
sustainably.
Every morning before you stand up from
bed, sit on the edge of your mattress
and lift your toes 10 times on each
foot.
This wakes up your dorsiflexors before
your feet ever touch the floor, which is
one of the most dangerous moments of
your entire day. Cold muscles, stiff
joints, and an unfocused mind all
combine in those first few steps out of
bed.
Warming those ankle muscles before you
stand gives you a critical layer of
protection right when you need it most.
Then once during the day, after lunch
works very well, complete your full five
exercise session using the repetitions
outlined above.
The entire session takes less than 10
minutes. Finally, in the evening do 10
slow high knee marches on each leg while
holding the back of a chair. This keeps
your hip flexors activated and your
lifting reflex sharp even on the days
when your full session is not possible.
Morning toe lifts, afternoon full
session, evening knee marches.
Three simple commitments, less than 15
minutes total, and over four to six
weeks the difference in how confidently
your feet interact with the ground
beneath them will genuinely surprise
you.
Important safety warning.
Before you begin any of these exercises,
please take this seriously because your
safety comes first, always.
If you have recently had hip surgery or
ankle surgery of any kind, please speak
with your doctor or physical therapist
before attempting these movements. Your
recovery may require a modified
approach.
Always keep one hand on a wall,
handrail, or sturdy chair throughout
every exercise. Never practice in socks
on a smooth floor. Wear non-slip shoes
or go barefoot on a safe surface.
If you feel sharp pain anywhere in your
hip, shin, or ankle, stop immediately.
If you feel sudden dizziness or any loss
of balance, sit down right away. There
is no repetition here that is worth
risking a fall. Move at your own pace
and trust your body to tell you when it
is ready for more.
