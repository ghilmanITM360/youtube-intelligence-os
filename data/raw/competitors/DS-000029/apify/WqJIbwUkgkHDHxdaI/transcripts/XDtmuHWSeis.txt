Why your ankles are making you fall.
Let me explain what's actually happening
inside your ankle because once you
understand this, the exercises I'm about
to show you will make complete sense.
Your ankle joint is surrounded by a
network of small but incredibly
important muscles, tendons, and
ligaments. Woven throughout that entire
structure are thousands of tiny sensory
receptors called proprioceptors.
Think of them as microscopic motion
sensors. Every millisecond they are
sending signals to your brain,
information about where your foot is,
how much weight is on it, which
direction the ground is pushing back,
and whether your body is shifting off
center. Your brain processes all of that
information in real time and sends back
instructions to keep you upright.
That system, when it's working well, is
extraordinary. It catches you before you
even know you're falling. It makes micro
corrections dozens of times per minute
automatically without any conscious
effort on your part.
But here is what happens after 70.
First, the muscles surrounding the ankle
weaken from years of walking on flat,
predictable surfaces, particularly if
you spend most of your time in thick,
cushioned shoes that do much of the
stability work for you. Second, those
proprioceptive sensors, those motion
detectors, become less sensitive with
age. The signals they send to your brain
slow down. And third, your brain's
ability to process those signals and
respond quickly also declines. The
result is a system that used to correct
a stumble in a fraction of a second that
now takes just a little too long to
react. And that small delay is all it
takes.
I describe it to my patients this way.
Imagine the shock absorbers on a car
that have been slowly wearing down for
years. The car still drives, it still
gets you where you're going, but every
bump in the road hits harder. Every
unexpected turn is a little less
controlled. And one day you hit a bump
that the old shock absorbers would have
handled easily, and now you don't.
That's your ankle after 70 without the
right training.
The critical thing I want you to hold on
to is this. Unlike cartilage, unlike
bone density, ankle strength and
proprioception respond remarkably well
to targeted exercise, even well into
your 80s and 90s. These are trainable
qualities. They are not permanent
losses. Your nervous system has not
forgotten how to do this. It simply
needs to be challenged consistently and
correctly to wake back up. That is
exactly what these five exercises will
do.
Exercise one, alphabet ankle trace.
Why it matters.
Most ankle exercises only move the foot
in one or two directions, but your ankle
needs to be strong and responsive in
every direction because falls come from
every direction.
The alphabet trace is one of the most
effective ways to take your ankle
through its full range of motion,
activating every muscle group
surrounding the joint and waking up the
proprioceptive sensors that protect you
in real life.
How to do it.
Sit in a sturdy chair with your feet
flat on the floor. Lift your right foot
a few inches off the ground. Using your
foot as a pin and your big toe as the
tip, slowly trace each letter of the
alphabet in the air, A B C and so on.
Make each letter as large as you
comfortably can. The movement should
come entirely from your ankle, not your
knee or hip.
Complete the full alphabet on the right
foot, then switch to the left.
What to focus on? Move slowly and with
intention. The larger and more
deliberate each letter, the more muscle
activation you get. If your ankle feels
stiff in the first few letters, that is
completely normal. The stiffness will
ease as you continue.
Progress tip. Over time, try doing this
exercise with your eyes closed. Removing
visual input forces your proprioceptive
system to work harder, accelerating the
neurological training.
Exercise two. Heel walking.
Why it matters.
The muscle that runs along the front of
your lower leg, the tibialis anterior,
is one of the most overlooked muscles in
fall prevention. Every time you lift
your foot to take a step, this muscle
controls how your foot rises and how it
lands. When it's weak, your foot tends
to drag slightly or land with a slap,
rather than a controlled placement. And
that is one of the leading causes of
trips and stumbles on uneven ground.
Heel walking directly targets this
muscle.
How to do it.
Stand behind a sturdy chair with your
hands resting lightly on the back for
support. Lift both sets of toes off the
ground so you are balancing entirely on
your heels. In this position, walk
forward slowly. 10 small steps on your
heels, keeping your toes raised the
entire time. Then walk backward 10
steps. That is one set. Complete two
sets.
What to focus on? Keep your steps small
and controlled. Do not rush. The slower
you move, the harder your tibialis
anterior has to work. Stand tall
throughout. Do not lean forward or let
your upper body compensate.
Progress tip. As this becomes easier,
try releasing your grip on the chair so
only your fingertips are touching it.
Eventually, work toward doing this
without any support at all. Arms out to
the sides for balance. Talk
Exercise three. Single leg ankle
circles.
Why it matters.
Standing on one leg while moving the
other is one of the most functional
balance challenges you can train because
it closely mimics what your body does
during every single step you take. For a
fraction of a second with every stride,
your entire body weight is balanced on
one foot. This exercise trains the ankle
of that standing leg to maintain
stability under load while also
improving the range of motion in the
working foot. A powerful combination for
fall prevention.
How to do it.
Stand next to a wall or chair for
support. Shift your weight onto your
right foot keeping a slight bend in the
right knee. Never lock it straight.
Slowly lift your left foot a few inches
off the ground.
Now, rotate your left foot in slow,
large circles. 10 circles clockwise,
then 10 circles counterclockwise.
Keep your standing right ankle as still
and stable as possible throughout. Then,
switch sides. Stand on your left foot
and circle your right.
Complete two rounds on each side.
What to focus on. The real training in
this exercise is happening in the
standing ankle, not the moving foot.
Focus on keeping your standing foot
stable, your weight evenly distributed,
and your standing knee slightly soft.
Breathe slowly and steadily.
Progress.
Tip. Once you feel steady, try taking
one finger off the wall instead of your
full hand, then two fingers. The goal
over time is to hover your hand near the
surface rather than gripping it.
Touching only if you genuinely need to.
Exercise four. Towel scrunch.
Why it matters.
The arch of your foot and the small
intrinsic muscles of your foot are
directly connected to your ankles
ability to stabilize. When these muscles
are weak, which happens after years of
supportive footwear and flat surfaces,
your entire foot collapses slightly
inward with each step, forcing your
ankle into a compromised position. The
towel scrunch reactivates these deep
foot muscles quickly and effectively,
restoring the foundation your ankle
depends on.
How to do it.
Sit in a sturdy chair and place a small
hand towel flat on the floor in front of
you. Place both feet on top of the towel
with bare feet. Using only your toes,
without lifting your heel from the
floor, scrunch the towel towards you,
gathering it under your feet. Then
spread your toes wide to release it and
smooth it flat again.
That is one repetition.
Complete 15 repetitions. Rest for 30
seconds, then complete a second set of
15
What to focus on. Keep your heels on the
floor at all times. The movement is
coming entirely from your toes and the
arch of your foot. You may feel mild
fatigue in the arch or the ball of your
foot. That is exactly the right muscles
working.
Progress tip. As this gets easier, try
doing it one foot at a time, which
significantly increases the challenge.
You can also progress to a thicker or
heavier towel to increase resistance.
Exercise five,
staggered stance hold.
Why it matters.
This final exercise is the one that ties
everything together. A staggered stance,
one foot slightly in front of the other,
is the position your body is in for a
significant portion of every step you
take. It is also one of the most
unstable positions for someone with weak
ankles. By holding this position and
allowing your ankle to make the small
continuous micro-corrections needed to
keep you upright, you are directly
training the real-time responsiveness
that prevents a stumble from becoming a
fall.
How to do it?
Stand next to a wall or chair for
safety. Place your right foot about half
a step in front of your left foot. Not a
full stride, just a short stagger. Both
feet should point forward. Your weight
should be distributed between both feet.
Hold this position for 20 to 30 seconds,
focusing on staying as still as possible
while letting your ankles make the small
adjustments they need to keep you
balanced. Then switch, left foot
forward, and hold again for 20 to 40
seconds.
Complete two holds on each side.
What to focus on? Let the wobbling
happen. Those small, subtle movements
you feel in your ankle are not a sign
that you are failing. They are the exact
training stimulus you need. Every
micro-correction your ankle makes is
your nervous system rebuilding its
responsiveness.
The goal is not to be perfectly still.
The goal is to stay upright while your
ankle does its work.
Progress tip.
Close your eyes once you feel steady in
this position. You will immediately
notice how much harder your ankles have
to work without visual input, and how
quickly your balance system adapts to
the challenge.
Important safety warning.
Now that you understand the five
exercises, please take a moment to hear
the safety warning before you actually
begin practicing them, because your
safety is the only thing that matters
here.
First, if you are currently being
treated for an ankle injury, ankle
fracture, Achilles tendon problem, or
any form of arthritis affecting your
foot or ankle joints, please speak with
your doctor or physical therapist before
attempting these movements. Some of
these exercises may need to be modified
or avoided entirely depending on your
specific condition.
Second, if you have been diagnosed with
peripheral neuropathy, a condition that
reduces sensation in your feet, please
proceed with extra caution and always
have a sturdy surface within reach.
Reduced sensation means reduced
feedback, which means balance challenges
can catch you off guard more easily.
Third, always do these exercises near a
wall, counter top, or sturdy chair. Not
because you expect to lose your balance,
but because having that safety net
nearby allows your nervous system to
relax, which actually makes the
exercises more effective.
Fourth, go barefoot if you can. For
ankle and foot training specifically,
direct contact with the floor
dramatically increases the sensory
feedback your brain receives, and that
feedback is the whole point of what
we're doing here. If you must wear
footwear, choose a thin-soled, non-slip
shoe. Never do these exercises in socks
on a hard floor.
And finally, if any movement causes
sharp pain, significant dizziness, or
anything that simply doesn't feel right,
stop immediately. There is no exercise
in this routine worth pushing through
pain for. Work within what feels safe
and comfortable for your body and
progress at your own pace.
