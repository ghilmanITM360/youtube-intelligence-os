Why turning is so dangerous.
Here's something most people don't
realize. Turning is one of the most
biomechanically complex movements we
make during normal daily activities.
When you're walking in a straight line,
your body settles into a rhythm. Your
weight is distributed evenly. Your
momentum is going in a predictable
direction, and your brain knows exactly
what to expect.
But the moment you turn, all of that
changes.
Your momentum is still carrying you
forward, but your body is trying to go
in a new direction. That mismatch
between where your momentum wants to
take you and where you're trying to go
creates instability.
Add to that the fact that your feet can
easily get into a tangled crossed
position during a turn, and you have a
recipe for a fall.
I've seen it happen in my clinic, and
I've heard the stories from patients
time and again. Someone is walking
through the kitchen, they turn to grab
something off the counter, their feet
get crossed, and down they go.
The two biggest turning mistakes I see
again and again are
mistake number one,
turning too tight.
This is when someone tries to spin
around on a single spot, almost like
they're trying to turn on a dime.
When you do this, your legs can easily
cross over each other. Your feet get
tangled. Your center of gravity shifts
to one side, and that's when a fall
happens.
I've heard from so many patients who
fell exactly this way.
They went to turn, their legs crossed,
and there was nothing they could do to
stop it.
Mistake number two, taking too big of a
step. Some people try to compensate for
tight turning by swinging one leg out in
a wide arc to change direction.
The problem here is that a large
crossover step throws your center of
gravity way outside of your body. Your
balance is compromised, and if you're
moving with any momentum at all, that
wide step can send you tumbling to one
side. This mistake is just as dangerous
as turning too tight, it just looks
different.
The solution to both of these problems
is the same, the marching turn.
The marching turn, your number one tool
for turning safely.
The marching turn sounds a little
unusual at first, but when you actually
do it, it feels natural. It looks
normal, and most importantly, it works.
Here's the concept. Instead of crossing
your legs over each other to turn,
either tightly or with a big step, you
use small controlled marching steps to
gradually rotate your body in the new
direction.
Here's how to practice it.
One, stand in a comfortable, stable
position with your feet about hip width
apart.
Two, decide you want to turn. Let's say
90° to your right.
Three, instead of swinging your right
foot across your body or pivoting
sharply, lift your right knee slightly.
Just a small controlled march step.
Four, as you step down, allow your body
to rotate about 30° to the right.
Five, lift the left knee, step, rotate
another 30°.
Six, continue this small marching motion
until you've completed your turn.
At no point in this movement do your
legs cross over each other. At no point
does your center of gravity shoot out to
one side. You are always in control,
always balanced, always safe.
Now, I'll be honest, when you first
practice this slowly, it might feel a
little exaggerated. But here's what I
found in 26 years of working with
patients. Once you speed it up to a
natural walking pace, it looks
completely normal. People around you
won't even notice. What they will notice
is that you're moving with confidence
and control.
The key things to remember about the
marching turn.
Step up and forward, not across. Your
feet should never cross the midline of
your body.
Rotate about 30° per step. Smaller turns
mean more steps, but more steps mean
more stability.
Use your ankle muscles.
Let your ankles and feet do the rotating
work, not your hips or momentum.
Take your time. There is no rush. A safe
turn is always better than a fast one.
Practice this turn in your home every
day.
Walk down the hallway, get to the end,
and use the marching turn to come back.
Do it in the kitchen. Do it in the
living room. The more you practice, the
more automatic it becomes, and the safer
you'll be.
Five supporting exercises to make
turning even safer.
The marching turn is your primary tool,
but like any tool, it works best when
the person using it is strong and
prepared. The following five exercises
are specifically designed to build the
strength, balance, and coordination that
makes safe turning possible.
Add these to your daily routine and
you'll be amazed at the difference they
make.
Exercise one, standing heel raises.
Why it matters. Your calf muscles and
ankles are your first line of defense
against falls. Strong ankles absorb
uneven ground,
adjust to changes in direction, and keep
you upright when your balance is
challenged. This exercise directly
targets that area.
How to do it.
Stand behind a sturdy chair and hold the
back lightly for balance.
Place your feet hip-width apart, toes
pointing forward.
Slowly rise up onto the balls of your
feet as high as you comfortably can.
Hold at the top for 2 seconds.
Slowly lower your heels back down to the
floor.
That's one rep. Complete two sets of 12
to 15 repetitions.
What to focus on.
Keep the movement slow and controlled,
especially on the way down. The lowering
phase is just as important as the
lifting phase for building ankle
strength.
Progress tip, as you get stronger, try
doing one leg at a time. Hold the chair
for safety and do eight to 10 reps on
each side.
Exercise two,
side-to-side weight shifts.
Why it matters. When you turn, your
weight shifts from one foot to the
other. If your body isn't practiced at
controlling that lateral shift, a turn
can easily throw you off balance. This
exercise trains your brain and your legs
to manage side-to-side weight changes
safely.
How to do it.
Stand with your feet shoulder-width
apart with a chair or countertop nearby
for support.
Slowly shift your weight to your right
foot, lifting your left foot slightly
off the ground, just a half inch or so.
Hold for 3 to 5 seconds, keeping your
right knee soft, not locked.
Slowly shift back to center, then to
your left foot.
Hold for 3 to 5 seconds.
That's one full rep. Complete two sets
of 10 repetitions on each side.
What to focus on. Keep your standing
knee slightly bent. Don't lock it. Think
about keeping your core gently engaged.
Imagine someone is about to tap your
shoulder from the side and you're
bracing yourself.
Progress tip. Try doing this with your
eyes closed once you feel steady.
Removing visual input forces your
balance system to work harder and get
stronger.
Exercise three, marching in place. Why
it matters. The marching turn depends on
your ability to lift your knees with
control while staying balanced. This
exercise builds exactly that skill. It
also improves hip flexor strength, leg
coordination, and the connection between
your brain and your feet, all of which
decline with age and must be actively
maintained.
How to do it. Stand behind a chair,
holding it lightly for balance.
Begin marching in place, lifting each
knee to a comfortable height, ideally to
about hip level, but go as high as is
comfortable for you.
March at a slow, controlled pace. Count
one full left-right cycle as one rep.
Complete two sets of 20 steps, 10 per
side.
What to focus on.
Don't rush. Keep your torso upright.
Don't lean forward or let your back
round. Each step should be deliberate
and controlled.
Progress
tip. Once you feel comfortable, practice
the full marching turn movement during
this exercise.
Walk forward a few steps, march turn to
the left or right, walk a few more
steps, turn again.
You're now practicing the exact skill
you need for daily life.
Exercise four, tandem standing,
tightrope balance. Why it matters. The
most vulnerable moment during a turn is
that split second when your weight is
transitioning from one foot to the other
on a narrow base.
Tandem standing, placing one foot
directly in front of the other, trains
your body to manage exactly this kind of
narrow, unstable base.
It's one of the most effective balance
exercises available and it requires no
equipment.
How to do it.
Stand next to a wall or counter top for
safety.
Place your right foot directly in front
of your left foot so that the heel of
your right foot is touching the toes of
your left foot. This is called the
tandem position.
Hold this position for 20 to 30 seconds,
trying not to touch the wall unless
necessary.
Switch so your left foot is in front and
hold again for 20 to 30 seconds.
Complete two sets on each side.
What to focus on. Keep your gaze fixed
on a spot on the wall directly in front
of you.
A steady visual focus point dramatically
improves balance. Breathe normally and
keep your knees slightly soft.
Progress tip. Try doing this with your
arms crossed over your chest rather than
extended for balance. This makes the
exercise harder by removing your arms as
a counter balance.
Exercise five, clock reach
exercise.
Why it matters. In real life, falls
don't happen in straight lines. They
happen when we reach, twist, and turn
all at the same time. The clock reach
exercise trains your balance under
exactly these multi-directional
conditions. It builds core stability,
hip strength, and the dynamic balance
that protects you during complex daily
movements like turning while carrying
something.
How to do it.
Stand on your right foot next to a chair
holding it lightly with your right hand
for safety.
Imagine you're standing in the center of
a clock face on the floor. 12:00 is
directly in front of you. 6:00 is
directly behind you.
Using your left hand, slowly reach
forward toward 12:00 as far as you
comfortably can, then bring your arm
back to center.
Reach to 10:00, slightly to the left,
return.
Reach to 9:00, directly to your side,
return.
Reach to 7:00, diagonally behind you,
return.
That's one full set. Complete two rounds
on each leg.
What to focus on. Move slowly and with
intention. Keep your standing knee
slightly bent. Let your hip and core
muscles do the work of keeping you
stable. This is the real training
happening in this exercise.
Progress tip. As you get stronger,
reduce your grip on the chair.
Eventually, try just hovering your
fingertips near the surface, only
touching if you actually start to lose
balance.
