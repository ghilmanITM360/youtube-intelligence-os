Acetylcholine is the neurotransmitter
most associated with memory formation,
attention, and learning. The hippocampus
and the surrounding memory centers in

medications, as the name implies, block
acetylcholine activity. They sit on the
receptors where acetylcholine would

from being transmitted. Different

appear in cold and allergy medications.
Some are used to relax an overactive
bladder. Some are used as sedatives for
sleep. Some are used as antidepressants.
The mechanism is the same in every case:
block acetylcholine somewhere in the
body. Here's the problem:
anticholinergic drugs are not selective.
A pill taken to dry up your nose doesn't

barrier and blocks acetylcholine in your
hippocampus, too. For a single dose, the
effect is temporary and minor. You might
feel slightly foggy, slightly drowsy.
For years of nightly use, the effect is
cumulative. The brain's acetylcholine
signaling is suppressed night after
night, year after year. In a younger
brain with robust acetylcholine
production, this suppression is
buffered. The brain produces more,
compensates, recovers each morning. In
an aging brain, acetylcholine production
has already declined. The drug's
suppression has a much larger relative
effect. The recovery is incomplete. Over
years, the hippocampus and memory
centers begin to physically atrophy. The
2015 study that quantified this was
