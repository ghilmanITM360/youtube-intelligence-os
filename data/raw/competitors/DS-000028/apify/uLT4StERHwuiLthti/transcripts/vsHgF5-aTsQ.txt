If your legs ache when you walk to the
mailbox and the pain fades the moment
you sit down, that is not aging. That is
your arteries warning you they are
closing. I am Dr. Marcus Acouro, board
certified cardiologist and heart
surgeon. In nearly 20 years of operating
on senior patients, I have learned that
the heart almost never sends its first
warning to the chest. It sends it to the
legs. The arteries in your calves and
feet are smaller, more delicate, and
they clog years before the arteries near
your heart do. By the time your chest
hurts, the damage has already been
spreading for a decade. Today, I'm going
to walk you through the seven warning
signs in your legs that mean your
arteries are dangerously blocked. If you
are over 60 and you have even two of
these, I want you to call your doctor
this week, not next month, this week.
Stay with me because the third sign is
the one most people dismiss as a hot
bath or cold weather, and it is the one
I see most often before a stroke. Howard
was 78 years old. He came into my clinic
last spring because his wife made him.
His complaint was simple. His right calf
cramped when he walked from his front
door to his car, about 40 yards. The
pain would go away if he stood still for
a minute. He'd been living with it for
almost 2 years. His primary doctor told
him it was probably arthritis, maybe a
pinched nerve. They gave him an
anti-inflammatory and sent him home.
When Howard sat down on my exam table, I
asked him to take off his shoes and
socks. His right foot was visibly paler
than his left, cooler to the touch. The
pulse on the top of his right foot,
gone. We ran a simple test called an
ankle-brachial index. His right leg had
less than half the blood flow of his
left. Howard had severe peripheral
artery disease in his right leg and a
70% blockage in the artery that feeds
his lower body. If we had waited 6 more
months, Howard could have lost his foot
or had a fatal heart attack. Because
here is the part nobody told Howard.
When your leg arteries are blocked, your
heart arteries usually are, too. The
seven signs I'm about to show you are
the same seven I look for on every
senior patient who walks into my clinic.
You can check most of them right now
sitting in your chair. Get a pen if you
need one. The first sign is pain or
cramping in your calf, thigh, or buttock
when you walk that stops within a few
minutes of resting. Doctors call this
intermittent claudication. The Latin
word claudicare means to limp. Here's
what is happening inside your leg. When
you walk, your calf muscles demand 4 to
10 times more oxygen than when you sit.
A healthy artery delivers that oxygen
instantly. A blocked artery cannot. The
muscle starves. It cramps. You sit.
Oxygen demand drops back to baseline.
The pain goes away. Most seniors blame
this on arthritis, a bad knee, a tight
muscle. The difference is this.
Arthritis pain hurts when you start
moving and gets better as you warm up.
Claudication is the opposite. It starts
a few minutes into walking and only rest
fixes it. If your pain has a predictable
distance, the mailbox, the end of the
block, one flight of stairs, that is a
vascular pattern, not a muscle pattern.
Write down the exact distance you can
walk before it starts. Your doctor will
need that number. Sign number two. One
foot consistently colder than the other.
Touch the top of both feet right now
with the back of your hand. They should
feel about the same temperature. If one
foot, usually the right, feels
noticeably cooler, even after you have
been sitting under a blanket, that is
reduced blood flow on that side. Cold
feet from cold weather affect both feet
equally. Cold feet from blocked arteries
are usually one-sided because
atherosclerosis rarely clogs both legs
at exactly the same rate. This is the
sign Howard had that nobody checked. His
wife actually noticed it months before I
did. She said his right foot was always
cold in bed, even in July. Sign number
three. This is the one I warned you
about. Lie flat on your back. Lift one
leg straight up in the air for about a
minute. Now look at the color of your
foot. A healthy leg stays pink. A leg
with blocked arteries turns pale, white,
sometimes even chalky. Now hang that leg
over the side of the bed. Watch what
happens. A blocked leg will slowly turn
a deep red, purple, or bluish color over
the the 30 to 60 seconds. This is called
dependent rubor. It happens because the
blood that struggled to reach your foot
finally pools there with gravity and the
vessels are so damaged they cannot
circulate it back. If you see this color
change in your own leg, please do not
wait. This is the body telling you the
arteries are critically narrow. People
dismiss it as a hot bath, sunburn or
eczema. It is none of those things. Sign
number four, cuts, blisters or sores on
your toes or feet that take weeks or
months to heal or do not heal at all.
Healthy skin closes a small cut in about
a week because oxygenated blood floods
the wound with healing cells. Blocked
arteries cannot deliver that supply. A
scrape on the back of the heel turns
into a black ulcer. A blister on the toe
goes from pink to gray. Diabetics are
especially vulnerable here, but so is
anyone with PAD. If you have a sore on
your foot or lower leg that has not
healed in 2 weeks, that is a medical
emergency in slow motion. Untreated,
these wounds are how seniors lose toes,
lose feet and eventually lose legs. Sign
number five, hair loss on your calves,
ankles or the tops of your toes. Hair
follicles need a steady oxygen supply to
grow. When blood flow drops, the
follicles starve and shut down. Most men
over 60 already have thinning leg hair,
so this can be tricky to spot. Compare
the two sides. If one calf is noticeably
smoother than the other, especially the
lower half, that is the side losing
circulation. Look at the tops of your
toes, too. Healthy toes have fine hair
on the upper joint. Blocked toes are
completely bald. This sign is quiet. It
does not hurt. It is one of the earliest
changes, sometimes years before the
cramps start. Sign number six, a weak or
absent pulse on top of your foot. You
can check this yourself in about 30
seconds. Place two fingers, index and
middle, on the top of your foot, roughly
in line with the gap between your big
toe and second toe, about an inch behind
the base of those toes. Press gently.
You are feeling for the dorsalis pedis
pulse. A healthy foot pulse is steady,
like a small drum. If you cannot find
it, try the inside of your ankle, just
behind the bony bump. That is the
posterior tibial pulse. If you cannot
find either pulse on one foot, but you
can't on the other, you have significant
arterial blockage on the missing side.
This is the same test I do on every
patient. It takes a doctor 20 seconds.
It can save your life. Sign number
seven, numbness, weakness, or burning
pain in your legs and feet, especially
when you lie down at night. Many seniors
blame this on neuropathy, and sometimes
that is correct. But here's the
giveaway, if your leg pain at night gets
better when you hang your foot off the
side of the bed, that is not neuropathy.
That is critical limb ischemia. Gravity
is helping blood reach a foot that
cannot get enough blood lying flat.
Patients tell me they end up sleeping in
a recliner. They wake up with throbbing
in their toes and have to stand up to
make it stop. Please do not normalize
this. This is end stage PAD, and it
means you are at very high risk of
losing the limb or having a major
cardiac event within the next year. Here
is what I want you to understand.
Blocked arteries in your legs are almost
never alone. They are a marker for
blocked arteries everywhere, your heart,
your brain, your kidneys. A patient with
severe peripheral artery disease has up
to a sixfold increased risk of dying
from a heart attack or stroke within
five years. That is not me being
dramatic. That is the data. So when you
find a sign in your leg, you are not
just protecting your foot. You are
protecting your heart, your brain, and
your independence. If you found two or
more of these signs in yourself, do
three things this week. First, call your
primary doctor and ask for an ankle
brachial index test. It is non-invasive,
takes 10 minutes, and most doctors can
order it the same day. Second, if you
smoke, stop. Smoking is the single
biggest accelerator of PAD I see in my
clinic. The narrowing literally speeds
up by years per pack. Third, start
walking. I know that sounds backwards if
walking causes the pain, but supervised
gentle walking, even just to the point
of mild discomfort, then resting, then
walking again, is one of the few
treatments proven to grow new tiny blood
vessels around the blockage. Your body
is willing to build a detour if you give
it a reason to. Let me give you all
seven again. Fast. One, leg cramp when
you walk that stops when you rest. Two,
one foot colder than the other. Three,
pale leg when raised, deep red when
lowered. Four, sores or cuts that will
not heal. Five, hair loss on the calves
or toes. Six, weak or missing pulse on
top of the foot. Seven, burning or
numbness at night that gets better when
you hang your foot off the bed. If you
found two or more, you are not imagining
it. Your arteries are speaking. Listen.
