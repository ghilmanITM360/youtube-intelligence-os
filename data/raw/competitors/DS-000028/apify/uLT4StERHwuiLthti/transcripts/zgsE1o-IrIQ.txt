physician. That is always the goal. Now,
let me start by dismantling the biggest
myth in cholesterol management. The myth
that your diet is the primary driver of
your cholesterol levels. Reason number
one, your liver makes most of your
cholesterol and your diet plays a much
smaller role than you were told. This is
the single most misunderstood fact about
cholesterol and it is the reason so many
people feel like failures when their
dietary changes do not move their
numbers. Your body produces
approximately 80 to 85% of the
cholesterol in your bloodstream on its
own. Your liver is a cholesterol
manufacturing factory that runs 24 hours
a day, 7 days a week. It produces
cholesterol because your body needs
cholesterol. Cholesterol is essential
for building cell membranes. It is
essential for producing hormones
including vitamin D and sex hormones
like testosterone and estrogen. It is
essential for making bile acids that
help you digest food. Cholesterol is not
a poison. It is a critical building
material that every cell in your body
requires. The remaining 15 to 20% of
your blood cholesterol comes from the
food you eat. That is it 15 to 20%. So
when you eliminate eggs, when you stop
eating shrimp, when you give up cheese
and butter, you are only addressing a
fraction of the total cholesterol in
your system. For some people, dietary
changes can make a noticeable
difference, perhaps a 5 to 10% reduction
in total cholesterol. But for many
others, especially those whose livers
are genetically programmed to produce
large amounts of cholesterol, the
dietary changes barely register on their
blood test. A landmark study published
in the American Journal of Clinical
Nutrition found that for the majority of
people, the liver compensates for
reduced dietary intake by simply
producing more cholesterol on its own.
Your liver has a thermostat. When less
cholesterol comes in from food, the
liver turns up its own production. The
net effect on your blood levels is often
surprisingly small. This is why you can
eat a perfect diet for 6 months, go to
your doctor, get your blood drawn, and
see numbers that have barely budged. It
is not because you cheated. It is not
because you failed. It is because your
liver was making up the difference the
entire time. Now, does this mean diet
does not matter at all? Absolutely not.
A heart-healthy diet has tremendous
benefits for your overall cardiovascular
health beyond just cholesterol numbers.
It reduces inflammation, improves blood
vessel function, helps manage blood
pressure and blood sugar. But if your
primary goal is to lower your LDL
cholesterol number on a blood test, you
need to understand that diet alone is
probably not going to get you there if
your liver is a high producer. And that
is not a personal failure. That is
genetics and biology. Reason number two,
chronic inflammation is secretly driving
your cholesterol up. This is the factor
that I believe is the most
underappreciated in all of cholesterol
management, and it is one that I wish
more doctors would explain to their
patients. Cholesterol is not just
floating around in your blood for no
reason. One of its primary functions is
to act as a repair molecule. When the
inner lining of your arteries, the
endothelium, is damaged, your body sends
cholesterol to the site of damage as
part of the repair process. Think of
cholesterol like the spackle you use to
fill a crack in a wall. The spackle is
not the problem. The crack is the
problem. The spackle is the response.
Now, what causes that arterial damage in
the first place? In most cases, the
answer is chronic lowgrade inflammation.
And chronic inflammation is
extraordinarily common in seniors. It
can be caused by dozens of factors
including excess visceral fat around
your organs, insulin resistance or
pre-diabetes,
gum disease, chronic stress, poor sleep
quality, and sedentary lifestyle. When
chronic inflammation is present in your
body, your arterial walls are constantly
being damaged at a microscopic level and
your liver responds by producing more
and more cholesterol to send to those
damage sites for repair. So, your LDL
goes up not because you ate too many
eggs, but because your body is trying to
fix damage. Research published in the
journal of the American College of
Cardiology has shown that markers of
chronic inflammation such as high
sensitivity C reactive protein known as
HSCP
are actually better predictors of
cardiovascular events than LDL
cholesterol alone in many patients. This
concept completely reframes how we
should think about cholesterol. Instead
of asking, "How do I lower my
cholesterol?" The more productive
question for many patients is, "What is
causing the inflammation that is making
my body produce so much cholesterol in
the first place?" Let me walk through
some of the most common inflammation
sources. Visceral fat. This is the fat
that wraps around your internal organs.
It functions almost like an endocrine
organ, continuously releasing
inflammatory chemicals called cytoines
into your bloodstream. You can be a
normal weight and still carry dangerous
visceral fat. Insulin resistance and
pre-diabetes. When your cells become
resistant to insulin, your blood sugar
stays elevated for longer. That chronic
elevation in blood sugar is directly
inflammatory to your blood vessel walls.
Studies show 40 to 50% of adults over 60
have some degree of this gum disease.
This one surprises everyone. Chronic
periodonal disease creates a persistent
source of bacterial infection. Research
has consistently linked periodonal
disease to increased cardiovascular
risk. Getting your gum disease treated
could be about lowering your cholesterol
and protecting your heart. Chronic poor
sleep. If you are consistently getting
less than 6 hours of sleep, your body
exists in a state of chronic
physiological stress. This stress
elevates cortisol, promotes
inflammation, and can directly increase
hpatic cholesterol production. Sleep
quality and heart health are deeply
interconnected. I had a patient, a
67year-old woman whose LDL cholesterol
was stubbornly elevated at 165 despite
taking a moderate dose statin and eating
a very clean diet. When we checked her
HSCP, it was significantly elevated. We
found untreated gum disease and early
insulin resistance. When we addressed
the gum disease and started managing her
insulin resistance, her HSCP came down
and her LDL dropped by an additional 25
points without any change to her statin
dose or diet. The inflammation was the
driver. The cholesterol was the
response. Ask your doctor for an HSCP
test. Reason number three, your thyroid
is quietly sabotaging your cholesterol
without you knowing it. This is one of
the most commonly overlooked causes of
high cholesterol in seniors and it is
one of the easiest to fix once it is
identified. Your thyroid gland controls
your metabolic rate. When your thyroid
is underactive, everything in your body
slows down, including the rate at which
your liver removes LDL cholesterol from
your bloodstream. The result is that LDL
accumulates because it is not being
cleared efficiently. Subclinical
hypothyroidism is common in seniors,
affecting 10 to 15% of adults over 60.
Symptoms are subtle, a little more
tired, a little more sensitive to cold.
Many people have no idea, but research
shows treating it can reduce LDL
cholesterol by 10 to 15%. It is like
trying to drain a bathtub with the
faucet still running. The statin was
pulling cholesterol out, but the
sluggish thyroid was preventing the
liver from doing its part of the
cleanup. I had a 71-year-old patient
whose LDL was stuck at 145 until we
found his thyroid was the issue. After
starting a low dose of thyroid
medication, his LDL dropped to 112 on
the same statin dose. If you have high
cholesterol, not responding to
treatment, ask your doctor for a
complete thyroid panel. TSH, free T4,
and free T3. This could be the hidden
answer. Reason number four, you might be
taking your statin at the wrong time of
day. This is a practical, immediately
actionable piece of information that
could improve the effectiveness of your
medication without changing anything
else. And most patients have never been
told about it. Not all statins are the
same. Some are short acting and others
are long acting. Because your liver does
most of its cholesterol production
during the night while you are sleeping,
the timing of a short acting statin
matters significantly. Syveastatin which
is zokor and lovastatin which is
mevacore are both short acting. If you
take them in the morning, the peak
activity occurs midday when production
is lowest. By nighttime the effect has
worn off. You are missing the hours when
you need it most. Research found that
taking Simba in the evening resulted in
significantly greater reductions in LDL
with some studies showing a 20 to 25%
difference in effectiveness simply from
changing the time of day the pill was
taken. That is a free and effortless
change. This doesn't apply to all
statins. Ottervastatin which is lipore
and rosastatin which is crestor are long
acting. They stay active for 20 hours or
more, so timing matters less, but
consistency is still key. Ask your
doctor if switching to evening dosing
might help you. Reason number five, your
doctor might be looking at the wrong
cholesterol number. This is the most
advanced concept I am going to discuss
today and it is transforming how
cardiologists think about cholesterol
risk. It is also one that most primary
care doctors are not yet routinely
using. For decades, we looked at LDL
cholesterol level or LDLC, which
measures the total amount of
cholesterol. But research shows it is
not the amount of cholesterol that
drives plaque. It is the number of LDL
particles themselves. This is called
LDLP or APOB. Imagine a highway.
Standard LDLC measures the total number
of passengers. LDLP measures the total
number of cars. If you have hundreds of
small cars carrying one passenger each,
you have more individual vehicles that
could crash and cause a blockage. More
particles mean more chances to penetrate
the artery wall. Having a normal LDLC,
but a high particle number is common in
people with insulin resistance,
metabolic syndrome, or diabetes.
Conditions prevalent in seniors. There
may be millions of seniors with normal
numbers who are actually at high risk.
Ask for an Apollo protein B or APOB
test. Now, let me bring all of this
