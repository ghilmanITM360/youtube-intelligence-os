This question comes from Sean, who asks: What 
if everything was antimatter, EXCEPT Earth?
This one doesn't end well for us. 
But—unlike most scenarios involving
the word "antimatter"—the end is 
surprisingly slow and drawn-out.
The whole universe is matter, as far as 
we can tell. No one is sure why there's
more matter than antimatter, since the 
laws of physics are pretty symmetrical,
and so there's no reason to expect 
there to be more of one than the other…
although when it comes down to it, there's 
no reason to expect anything at all.
It's possible that some 
galaxies are made of antimatter,
and we just haven't noticed because 
we haven't tried to touch them.
This is a cool idea, but if there are zones of 
matter and zones of antimatter in the universe,
we should see a telltale gamma-ray glow from
matter-antimatter annihilations along 
the boundaries between the zones.
So far, we haven't seen that anywhere nearby, 
although another telescope might help.
If the entire rest of the universe were swapped 
out for antimatter, then we'd be in trouble.
Outer space isn't really 
"space"; it's full of a thin gas.
Ok, technically, a plasma.
Ok, technically, there's also a substantial 
quantity of solid grains of dust.
Look, there's a bunch of little bits 
of stuff that are hard to see, ok?
Ok, except when they're not hard to see.
And, if they’re all swapped for 
antimatter, we’re in trouble.
The Earth's magnetic field 
protects us from the solar wind,
and would protect us from an anti-solar wind, too.
A tiny fraction of the particles 
from the Sun do reach the Earth,
funneled down by our magnetic 
field, and create the aurora.
In this scenario, the aurora would get a lot 
brighter, but most of the time not bright
enough to really cause problems.
Meteors would be the real problem.
The Earth sweeps up space dust as it travels 
around its orbit. Somewhere around 30 tons of
dust per day enters the atmosphere in the form 
of tiny grains, most weighing about 10^-5 grams.
An additional similar average per-day 
amount arrives in giant clumps all at once.
This inflow of antimatter micrometeor dust would
collide with the top of our matter 
atmosphere and be annihilated.
The interactions between the nucleons 
and antinucleons would be complex,
but the end result would be a lot 
of neutrinos which would fly away,
and a lot of gamma rays, which 
would turn into a lot of heat.
This steady flow of material 
would be worst around dawn,
when your house was facing in 
the direction of Earth's motion.
The heat and light added by the antimatter 
would certainly be enough to tip the Earth
into a "runaway greenhouse" scenario, quickly 
turning the Earth into something resembling Venus.
But the big antimatter meteors would get us first.
Even a relatively small antimatter object 
similar in size to the Chelyabinsk meteor
would deliver as much energy as the 
matter meteor that killed the dinosaurs.
Meteors the size of Chelyabinsk’s hit the 
earth roughly every 50 years, but slightly
smaller ones enter the atmosphere 
every few months—mostly unnoticed.
If they were all antimatter, each one 
would trigger a tremendous pulse of
energy in the sky and ignite a massive firestorm.
Right now, it's still an open question whether any
significant non-zero percentage of the 
stuff in the sky is made of antimatter.
It's probably not, but we'd need to build another 
orbiting gamma-ray telescope to really be sure.
However, it's easy to use a telescope 
to rule out one possibility:
That everything in the sky 
is antimatter – if it were,
your telescope (and everything 
else) would be on fire.
If you have a telescope that’s not on fire, 
maybe you can get that result published.
