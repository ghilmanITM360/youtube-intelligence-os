If the Hubble telescope were aimed at the 
Earth, how detailed would the images be?
The fundamental problem is that 
Hubble is too slow. Let me explain.
Hubble routinely swivels to follow 
targets as it orbits. Usually,
the targets are astronomical objects that 
are super far away – the automatic on-board
tracking algorithms can at best handle 
targets as close as about ten times the
distance to the Moon. When astronomers want to 
use Hubble to take pictures of the Moon itself,
they have to send it manual commands to rotate
It doesn’t work very well. The resulting
photos are better than what you can 
see with the naked eye, but they’re
not very good by Hubble standards.
To track the surface of the Earth,
Hubble would need to rotate less than one degree 
per second, or around one tenth of an rpm.
But Hubble is simply too slow – it 
wasn’t built for surface tracking.
Clearly, we need our own targeting control 
system. With our custom system in place,
Hubble could be made to track the surface and 
get photos pretty close to its optimal quality.
While Hubble couldn’t read over your shoulder, it 
could watch you roll around on your desk chair.
But this isn’t just a hypothetical situation. Much 
of the technology in military spy satellites is
believed to be similar to that of Hubble. So in 
a sense, pointing a Hubble-type telescope at the
earth’s surface is not only possible—it’s 
what the US government actually does.
